Names

Spotted Salamander, Ambystoma maculatum.

Range and habitat

Adults feed and overwinter in upland hardwood and mixed forests, spending most of the year underground in shrew, mole or mouse tunnels and wintering in deeper tunnels below the frost line. Breeding sites are vernal pools, beaver ponds or old farm ponds (Atlas). The state page lists it among the "pool-specialist" amphibians with the wood frog, Jefferson salamander and blue-spotted salamander (Wood Frog).

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Size: the largest of Vermont's three mole salamanders; large adults average 8.5 inches (Atlas).
  • Color and shape: a black salamander with bright yellow spots, strong legs and a broad, flat head.
  • Eggs and larvae (state page): about 150 eggs per mass, one or two months to hatch, and 70 to 100 days as aquatic larvae before the young leave the water.

Role and management

Vermont Fish & Wildlife says salamanders eat beetles, earthworms, slugs, spiders, mites and mosquito larvae. Neither page gives landowner advice beyond asking for sighting reports. For vernal pool buffers and harvest advice, see the Wood Frog note, which records the state's fact-sheet recommendations (Wood Frog, R-VT-004 Vernal Pool ESTA).

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

  • Water, shade and cover (the Guide, chapter 30). Most reptiles and amphibians need water for some stage of their life: conserve streams, ponds, wetlands and vernal pools. Frogs, toads and most salamanders lay eggs in water; many breed in temporary vernal pools free of fish, where the young change from egg to larva to adult in one summer if the pool stays wet (dry years give few eggs or hatchlings). Keep intact forest buffers along water, rock walls, brush piles, downed trees and hollow stumps; leave intact leaf litter and rotten logs; leave coarse woody debris when working in the woods ('woods that look like city parks are not good habitat'); keep streams shaded and free of sediment with a naturally vegetated buffer for night-time feeding; abundant shade keeps the woods cool and moist.
  • This species: a salamander of woodland vernal pools; terrestrial salamanders feed during a few warm, wet nights and seek leaf mold, decaying logs and moist spots under rocks (Guide). For vernal pool buffers see Wood Frog.

Legal status

Not stated on the pages read for this species (the SGCN label is a planning category, not a legal protection). The Wood Frog note records that the Vermont Wetland Rules protect vernal pools.
