Names

Hairy Woodpecker, Picoides villosus.

Range and habitat

The Atlas profile says the Hairy Woodpecker nests deep within large tracts of mature forest and benefits from forest regrowth and maturation. It names no formal threats; it notes only that birds can become a nuisance by excavating cavities in house siding. The Forest Bird Monitoring Program trend is an annual decline of 0.8 percent.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Size: 7.1 to 10.2 inches long (18 to 26 cm), wingspan 13 to 16.1 inches; about the size of a robin (Audubon).
  • Marks: black and white with a white stripe down the center of the back, checkered black wings and clean white outer tail feathers; the male has a red patch on the back of the crown. The bill is nearly as long as its head, which best separates it from the Downy (Downy Woodpecker).
  • Voice: a sharp "peek" that is louder than the Downy's, plus a loud rattle on one pitch.

Role and management

Audubon lists mostly insects, especially wood-boring beetle larvae, plus some berries, seeds, nuts, sap and suet at feeders. The Atlas gives no management recommendation.

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

  • Dead and dying trees. Audubon's diet lines for this species (as recorded in Role above) are mostly insects such as beetles and ants, and the department's targets for cavity and snag trees below serve all the woodpeckers; the Atlas gives no management recommendation for this species.
  • Cavity trees (the Guide, den-tree section). Manage for at least six cavity, snag and decadent living trees per acre on average, with one over 18 inches across at breast height and three over 16 inches; leave a selection of different diameters; try to leave two or more large den trees per acre within 300 feet of lakes and ponds; if a den tree or snag must be felled for safety, leave it lying as downed wood. Pileated Woodpecker records the department's targets in full.

Legal status

Native birds are covered by the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which prohibits taking (killing, capturing, selling, trading and transport are named) protected migratory birds without prior authorization (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service page, read through a summarizing fetch 2026-10-31). The Act applies to native species named in the treaties (50 CFR 10.13); that list was not checked for this species, and the page read says nothing about nests or about cutting trees in nesting season. Ask the county forester or the Fish and Wildlife Service before work near an active nest.
