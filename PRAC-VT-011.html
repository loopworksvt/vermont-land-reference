Pollinator habitat on forest and field

practicehabitat

How a landowner can keep bees and other pollinators on the land. The guide's main point: managing for pollinators uses many of the same practices as managing for other wildlife, and habitat diversity gives a diversity of bees.

Topic
habitat
Who is involved
landowner, consulting forester, logger

When it fits

Any parcel; especially where the owner wants more fruit (apples, blueberries, serviceberry), healthier wildlife food plants, or to support the at-risk bees (Bumble Bees, Yellow-banded Bumble Bee, Solitary and Other Native Bees).

How it is done

  • Keep a patchwork of habitats so bees have flowers all season: mature forest, forest with sunny openings, working wetlands, old fields with a mix of flowering forbs and shrubs, and fields not heavily cropped for corn or hay. Keep patches of flowers on the edges of fields and lawns and in hedgerows.
  • Plants bees favor (the Guide): goldenrod, aster and sunflowers, willows, the blueberry family (blueberries, cranberries, maleberry), dogwood, spring beauty, native loosestrifes and pickerel weed. Maples, willows and apples are important early-spring food. Some invasive plants attract bees (purple loosestrife) but others crowd out the native flowers bees need (Shrub Honeysuckles, Buckthorns, Garlic Mustard).
  • Nesting places: leave bare or sparsely vegetated, uncompacted soil in hedgerows, dry banks and forest roads; do not till everything (leave some fallow); keep dead wood, pithy plant stems, sumac and logging slash where cavity nesters can use them; when releasing apple trees leave some dead wood; predrilled wood blocks and shoebox-sized wooden boxes (for bumble bees) can add nest sites.
  • Pesticides: widespread use is one of the greatest threats. Of particular concern are pesticides applied as dusts or small pellets, those applied to flowering plants, and neonicotinoids. Limit them in farm and other settings.
  • Watch the bees: note from year to year how many bees visit your plants. Poor fruiting of apples, blueberries and other plants may signal a need for more pollinator habitat. Learn a few of the obvious pollinators (Bumble Bees).

Who does what

The owner decides how much to leave and plant; the forester can retain dead wood and sunny openings in a harvest plan; the logger leaves slash where agreed; see Wildlife Habitat in Harvest Planning.

Timing

Early spring flowers (maples, willows, apples) feed bees first; nests are used through the season and overwintering sites matter in winter. Avoid soil disturbance and pesticide application where bees nest or forage in flower.

Costs and benefits

The guide gives no costs. Benefits: better fruit and seed set on bee-pollinated plants that feed bear, deer, turkey and moose; support for declining bumble bees. The cost is mostly leaving things alone (dead wood, bare banks, fallow patches) and limiting pesticide use.

Risks and what to avoid

Tilling nesting ground; clearing all dead wood; spraying flowering plants; removing flowering shrubs; planting or spreading invasive flowering plants.

Rules that apply

Applying pesticides may require certification from the Agency of Agriculture, Food and Markets (VTinvasives pages); some native bees are state-listed (Legal Checks Before a Harvest for the rare species rule).
