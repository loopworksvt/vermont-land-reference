Names

Savannah Sparrow, Passerculus sandwichensis.

Range and habitat

The Atlas profile calls it a grassland-obligate bird that nests everywhere from lightly grazed pastures to intensively cut hayfields and overgrown meadows; it benefited from 18th-century land clearing. Frequent early hay cutting is the main hazard: on intensive dairy hayfields females can lay up to six clutches (24 eggs) and fledge no young. It is most prevalent in the central and northern parts of the state, including the Champlain Valley.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Size: 4.7 to 5.9 inches long (12 to 15 cm), wingspan 7.9 to 9.4 inches; about sparrow-sized (Audubon).
  • Marks: a heavily streaked chest, a narrow white central crown stripe, a strong face pattern and often yellow in front of the eye; pointed wings.
  • Voice: a high-pitched, buzzy song that ends in a falling, trilled note.

Role and management

The Atlas says adults eat mostly insects, with seeds, particularly dandelion, in early summer, and that nestlings are fed only insects. Its profile points private landowners to the USDA Grassland Reserve Program for grassland bird management guidelines. See Bobolink and Eastern Meadowlark for the same mowing calendar.

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

Grassland birds in general (the Guide, chapter 15). They need large, grass-dominated fields with other herbaceous plants, few woody plants and an open landscape: fields over 25 acres are most productive and as small as 10 acres will support some birds; round or square fields give more interior than long, narrow ones; 50 to 75 percent grass with the rest forbs; no shrubs such as dogwood, alder and cherry; and a period each summer with no mowing, because birds arrive in early May and the young need 49 to 52 days to escape mowers, with a field in use until early to mid-August.

  • This species: it nests in the same fields as the bobolink and meadowlark and has the same mowing problem (Bobolink, Eastern Meadowlark); the Atlas points private landowners to the USDA Grassland Reserve Program for grassland bird guidelines.

Legal status

Native birds are covered by the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which prohibits taking (killing, capturing, selling, trading and transport are named) protected migratory birds without prior authorization (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service page, read through a summarizing fetch 2026-10-31). The Act applies to native species named in the treaties (50 CFR 10.13); that list was not checked for this species, and the page read says nothing about nests or about cutting trees in nesting season. Ask the county forester or the Fish and Wildlife Service before work near an active nest.
