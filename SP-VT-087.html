Legal status
State ranks S5B and S3N; no specific recommendations. The Vermont Fish & Wildlife page calls it a summer resident, abundant from April through October. The Atlas says the population has leveled off, and some researchers suspect West Nile virus. Federal law: the Migratory Bird Treaty Act (see Legal status). (checked 2026-10-31)

Names

American Robin, Turdus migratorius.

Range and habitat

The Atlas says robins favor forest edges, clearings, road cuts, lawns and hedgerows, and in autumn move south in large flocks and form winter roosts. Vermont Fish & Wildlife says it originally nested in forests but now also uses towns, gardens, open woods and farmland, needs open ground for foraging plus trees and shrubs for nesting and roosting, and decreases in abundance with increasing elevation.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Size: 8 to 11 inches long (20 to 28 cm), wingspan 12 to 16 inches (Audubon).
  • Marks: a brick-red chest, a gray back and a white chin with streaks; males have a blacker head. Small white spots around the eyes and usually on the tail corners. Juveniles are heavily spotted below and show a reddish tinge on the chest.
  • Voice: a series of rich, caroling notes that rise and fall in pitch, often sung at dawn.

Role and management

Vermont Fish & Wildlife lists earthworms, caterpillars, beetles, fruits and berries, and says fruit makes up the greater share of the diet; Audubon puts fruit at about 60 percent over the year. The state's page suggests planting berry bushes, offering raisins, grapes or suet on a platform feeder, and leaving a wet area or small brush pile to encourage invertebrates.

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

  • Berries, soft ground and a wet spot. Fruit makes up the greater share of the diet; Vermont Fish & Wildlife suggests planting berry bushes, offering raisins, grapes or suet on a platform feeder, and leaving a wet area or small brush pile to encourage invertebrates (as recorded in the note).
  • Food trees: the Guide's chapter on shrubland and young forest (chapter 7) says to cut selectively to promote trees that are food sources for wildlife, such as beech, oak, cherry and apple.

Legal status

Native birds are covered by the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which prohibits taking (killing, capturing, selling, trading and transport are named) protected migratory birds without prior authorization (U.S. The Act applies to native species named in the treaties (50 CFR 10.13). Ask the county forester or the Fish and Wildlife Service before work near an active nest.
