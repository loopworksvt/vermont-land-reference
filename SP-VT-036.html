Names

Kentucky bluegrass, Poa pratensis, a grass in the family Poaceae.

Range and habitat

The Vermont plants are not native. Go Botany says two subspecies are rare natives (one only on Mt. Washington, New Hampshire; one only in northern Maine) and two are widespread non-natives; the two known from Vermont, pratensis and angustifolia, are the non-native ones. It grows in meadows and fields, lawns, roadsides and other disturbed or maintained places. The fire-effects page says it grows best on moist sites in cool, humid climates and on well-drained loams, and that it is most common in the northern half of the United States.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Growth habit (UVM): a short, low-growing perennial that forms a dense sod and spreads by many short rhizomes. Kentucky bluegrass is dark green; Canada bluegrass is blue-green. Leaves fold in the bud shoot.
  • Leaf: narrow, with a boat-shaped tip and a midrib with two grooves that is translucent when held to the light (UVM). Blades 0.4 to 4.5 mm wide (Go Botany). Ligule short and blunt (truncate, UVM), 0.9 to 3.1 mm; no auricles.
  • Seedhead: a branched panicle with very small seeds (UVM), 30 to 200 mm long, each branch with 5 to 15 spikelets of 2 to 5 florets (Go Botany).
  • Height: 8 to 100 cm (Go Botany).
  • Look-alikes (UVM): Canada bluegrass (blue-green, also truncate ligule). Annual bluegrass is an annual bunchgrass with a pointed ligule and crinkled leaves, found near gates and laneways where soil is compacted. Roughstalk bluegrass has a pointed ligule, a rough surface, a loose green panicle, spreads by stolons and prefers shady, moist places.

Role in the field

The fire-effects page calls it extremely competitive: a vigorous herbaceous competitor that spreads by rhizomes, makes abundant seed, is highly resistant to grazing, and is persistent once dominant. It says Kentucky bluegrass increases rapidly on overgrazed pastures and that its presence usually indicates past poor grazing management, and it is considered an invader in hay mixtures. Those statements are general and mostly from prairies; the page gives no eastern details.

What helps it thrive (light, soil and moisture, and how it is kept)

  • Light: grows best in full sunlight but tolerates light shading if moisture and nutrients are favorable (FEIS).
  • Soil and water: most abundant on moist sites in cool, humid climates; thrives on well-drained loams or clay loams rich in humus, with a pH between 5.8 and 8.2; needs large amounts of nitrogen; intolerant of prolonged flooding, high water tables or poor drainage.
  • Keeping it: it often takes two or three years to establish and then persists as a dense sod; highly resistant to grazing; late spring mowing and raking, which simulates burning, reduces it, and frequent late spring burning controls it.
  • Use to others: good cover for small mammals and nongame birds.

Managing for it and living with it

The sources read give no mowing advice for Kentucky bluegrass in a Vermont field. The fire-effects page reports that, on tallgrass prairie, combined late-spring mowing and raking reduced it; that is a prairie result and the place is not named.

Legal status

Not on the Agency of Agriculture quarantine list as read on 2026-10-30. No other legal status was looked up.
