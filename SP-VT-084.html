Names

Eastern Bluebird, Sialia sialis.

Range and habitat

The Atlas says bluebirds nest in cavities and forage over open ground, which hedgerows and fencerows once supplied together. Their decline followed competition from non-native European Starlings and House Sparrows for nest holes, and farmland reverting to forest. Volunteer-maintained nest box trails now supply most of the state's nest sites. Audubon lists open country with scattered trees, including farms, roadsides, and suburbs with extensive lawns.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Size: 6 to 8 inches long (15 to 20 cm), wingspan 10 to 13 inches (Audubon).
  • Male: bright blue above, with a reddish-brown throat and chest and a white belly. Female: paler and duller. Juveniles: heavily spotted with traces of blue above.
  • Voice: a soft, melodious warble; the call is a liquid "turee" or "queedle".

Role and management

Audubon lists mostly insects and berries, with spiders, earthworms and snails, and says berries are especially important in winter. A high percentage of North American Eastern Bluebirds nest in birdhouses, often along "bluebird trails".

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

  • Nest cavities, and open ground to hunt over. The Guide says bluebirds benefit from grasslands but may also need cavity trees or nest boxes for nesting (chapter 15). Audubon says a high percentage of North American bluebirds nest in birdhouses, often along bluebird trails.
  • Food: insects and berries, with spiders, earthworms and snails; berries are especially important in winter (Audubon).
  • What to do: the Guide names cavity trees and nest boxes as what it may need; the sources read do not give box dimensions or placement.

Legal status

Native birds are covered by the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which prohibits taking (killing, capturing, selling, trading and transport are named) protected migratory birds without prior authorization (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service page, read through a summarizing fetch 2026-10-31). The Act applies to native species named in the treaties (50 CFR 10.13); that list was not checked for this species, and the page read says nothing about nests or about cutting trees in nesting season. Ask the county forester or the Fish and Wildlife Service before work near an active nest.
