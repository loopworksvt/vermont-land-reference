Specimen trees (what the term means, and the related terms foresters use)

practiceretention

In forest management the same idea goes by other names (legacy tree, retention tree, wildlife or den tree, mast tree, an 'aesthetic crop tree', a 'standard'), and a forester should be asked which of these is meant before a tree is marked to keep. This note lists the definitions found, so that you can say in a prescription or a sale contract exactly what is to be kept and why.

Topic
retention
Who is involved
landowner, county forester, consulting forester

When it fits

When a landowner wants to keep a particular tree, or a few, out of a harvest or a release: a very large or old tree, one of unusual form, a tree with a history, a good mast tree, a tree with cavities, or a tree that shades a house or a view. Say so before the forester writes the prescription.

What the definitions say

In local ordinances and statutes (ten definitions collected by Law Insider, read 2026-11-01; they differ, and none is a forestry rule):

  • Montgomery County, Maryland: a tree that is a particularly impressive or unusual example of its species, judged by size, shape, age or another trait.
  • Virginia (statute) and Arlington County, Virginia: a tree individually designated by the local governing body for its notable size and quality within its species.
  • A county infrastructure regulation (eng.hctx.net): an exemplary tree of good health and true to its species' habit and form, with a minimum trunk caliper of 1.5 inches.
  • A tree of exceptional size, form or rarity (a city archive); a tree or group of trees an urban forester finds highly valuable for species, size, age, form or historical significance.
  • Pasadena: trees meeting a species and size list set by the city council, presumed to have distinctive form, size or age.
  • Anaheim: oaks with a trunk at least 25 inches around, or pepper and sycamore trees at least 50 inches, measured 4 feet above the ground.
  • Wisconsin (statutes, Chapter 84): a live tree with a trunk at least 12 inches across, measured 3 feet above the ground.

So when a definition has a number, it is a trunk size that varies by place and by species, and most definitions have no number and rely on the word 'exceptional'. No Vermont definition was found.

In forest management, the nearest terms:

  • Legacy tree. Northern Woodlands: 'trees of an older generation that persist in a younger forest'.
  • Retention tree. In Vermont's state-land marking procedure, a 'Mark to Leave' job marks the 'retention trees' to keep; for example, where a harvest leaves a low-density stand, retention trees are marked with paint rather than the cut trees (Marking Trees for Harvest).
  • Aesthetic crop tree. The USDA Forest Service crop tree guide groups crop trees into timber, wildlife and aesthetic, and lists for the aesthetic ones fall foliage, spring blossoms and 'size, shape or form'; a single tree can be in two or all three groups (Crop Tree Release).
  • Wildlife, mast, cavity and den trees. The Michigan guide's preferred retention trees include large or 'super-canopy' trees, live cavity trees over 10 inches across, snags that are not a safety risk, and mast trees over 10 inches (hickory, oak, beech, black cherry, basswood and ironwood in that order of preference). The Vermont Landowner's Guide asks for at least six cavity, snag or decadent living trees per acre on average, one over 18 inches across and three over 16, and two or more large den trees per acre within 300 feet of lakes and ponds (Pileated Woodpecker records the targets).

How it is done (keeping them)

  • Choose and name the trees. Northern Woodlands suggests a range of species for diversity; large trees with unusual features are good candidates (its examples: a massive yellow birch with a crooked trunk, an old red maple riddled with cavities), and healthy valuable trees can occasionally be kept too. Old New England forests typically have only 10 to 12 big old trees per acre, and even two or three per acre is described as an asset.
  • How many. The sources give no 'right' number of specimen trees. Michigan's guidance for harvests generally is to retain 3 to 10 percent of the stand area or basal area (patches or trees); Northern Woodlands' figures are above.
  • For how long. Michigan: until at least the next cutting cycle, which in northern hardwood partial cuts is typically 10 to 20 years; for clearcuts until the next rotation, 40 years or more. Northern Woodlands' legacy trees are kept 'in perpetuity', to grow, seed and die.
  • Mark them. In Vermont's state procedure a leave tree in a Mark to Cut job gets an 'L' and no stump mark, and in a Mark to Leave job a horizontal stripe in an alternate color with a stump mark; the Northern Woodlands author marks each legacy tree with a bright blue L so future managers know it is grown for ecological benefit and can see how many there are. Write the same code into the contract.
  • What you give up. Legacy trees use space, light and water that could grow higher-value trees; Northern Woodlands says the cost is often small and sometimes a real compromise.

Who does what

The owner decides which trees are special and says why. The forester writes them into the prescription and the contract and marks them; the logger follows the marks (Selling Timber and Working With Professionals). On enrolled land, tell the county forester, who monitors compliance with the plan (Legal Checks Before a Harvest).

Timing

Name and mark specimen trees before the harvest or release is laid out and before the contract is signed; the Vermont procedure says the way trees to be kept are designated must be spelled out in the prospectus and contract and again at the showing and pre-sale conference. Marks must still be visible when the logger arrives (see Marking Trees for Harvest).

Costs and benefits

Cost: the timber value of a tree left standing, and the trees it shades (small in most stands, per Northern Woodlands). Benefit: large canopies make mast and give foraging, nesting and denning places; bark supports mosses, lichens and bark-feeding birds; decaying trees feed decomposers and make cavities (Northern Woodlands).

Risks and what to avoid

Using the word 'specimen' in a contract without saying which tree it is, in what color and how many; leaving a tree with defects that could fall on a road, building or trail (Michigan retains snags only if they are not a safety risk); assuming a tree that looks special will be skipped by a crew.

Rules that apply

Local tree ordinances (the definitions above) apply to town and city land. Current Use forest management is guided by the plan the county forester approves.
