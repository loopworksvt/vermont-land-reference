Names

Red clover, Trifolium pratense, in the pea family, Fabaceae.

Range and habitat

Not native: Go Botany answers "No" to native to North America and records the plant as present in Vermont. It lists human-disturbed areas, meadows and fields, and shores of rivers or lakes, and marks it as usually in non-wetlands (FACU). Minnesota Wildflowers gives origin as Europe, Asia and Africa and lists sun; fields and along roads.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Height: 40 to 100 cm.
  • Flowers: pink-red or white, 13 to 20 mm long, 30 to 70 in a rounded head 20 to 40 mm long and 20 to 30 mm wide.
  • Leaves: compound and alternate with three leaflets 10 to 60 mm long and 10 to 20 mm wide; the upper surface has obvious spots, mottles or stripes. The stipules (leaf-base wings) are triangular, 10 to 30 mm long.
  • Look-alikes (Go Botany): Trifolium dalmaticum and zigzag clover (Trifolium medium), named without a comparison.

What helps it thrive (light, soil and moisture, and how it is kept)

  • Light: good shade tolerance (about 6 percent of daylight), which is why it can be sown under a companion crop (USDA NRCS Plant Guide).
  • Soil and water: grows best on well-drained loamy soils but will also grow on soil that is not as well drained; medium and fine textures are preferred over sandy or gravelly soils; best adapted to a pH of 6.0 or higher but grows on moderately acid soils; needs enough moisture all season (mean annual precipitation 25 inches or more).
  • Life and spread: an introduced biennial or short-lived perennial, productive only about three years; it fixes nitrogen when properly inoculated and needs phosphorus, potassium and sulphur in some areas.
  • Cutting and grazing: cut or graze at one-quarter to one-half bloom, a second cutting at one-quarter bloom, and leave at least 2 inches of growth after each harvest; it can cause livestock to bloat.
  • Use to others: bumble bees are especially important pollinators (honey bees prefer sweet, white and alsike clover); the guide says it is not particularly noted for wildlife cover or food, though many wildlife species use legumes for the early green-up (Pollinator Habitat on Forest and Field).
