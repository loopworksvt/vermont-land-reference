Names

Ovenbird, Seiurus aurocapilla.

Range and habitat

The Atlas says the Ovenbird prefers large tracts (more than 100 hectares, 247 acres) of mature deciduous or mixed deciduous forest with a fairly closed canopy (60 to 90 percent) and an open understory. Smaller patches in a fragmented landscape may be occupied, but productivity is lower. It is also found in the pole-size hardwood forests that grow when old fields are left uncut. It is found throughout the state, slightly less common in the Champlain Valley, and Breeding Bird Survey data show a significant increase of 1.9 percent a year from 1982 to 2007.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Size: 5.5 to 6.3 inches long (14 to 16 cm), wingspan 7.9 to 9.8 inches (Audubon).
  • Marks: an olive-brown back, a striped chest, a dull orange crown stripe bordered by blackish stripes and a bold white eye-ring. It walks deliberately on the ground with its tail held up.
  • Voice: a loud, staccato "teacher, teacher, teacher"; the flight song is a bubbling jumble ending with the same phrase, often given at night.
  • Look-alikes (Audubon): brown thrushes, which lack the crown stripe.

Role and management

Audubon lists mostly insects such as beetles, ants and caterpillars, plus worms, spiders and snails, and describes mature forest with a closed canopy, large trees and sparse ground cover. The Atlas says the mature forest it prefers is still increasing and gives no management recommendation; its habitat figures (large blocks, closed canopy, open understory) are the facts to weigh when planning a cut in a large woodland.

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

Forest songbirds in general (the Guide, chapter 6). Keeping forest intact is possibly the most important thing: large patches not cut up by roads, buildings or big openings hold more forest birds and produce more young, and an opening changes the forest for more than 200 meters from its edge (more nest predators and cowbirds in small or fragmented patches of under 30 acres). Keep a variety of habitats (hardwood, softwood, wetland, floodplain) and stream and wetland buffers; keep some old forest (over 100 years) with cavity trees and dead wood, and where a mostly forested landscape has under 5 percent young forest consider 1- to 5-acre patches of young forest. Different species use different layers: ovenbirds nest on the leaf litter, black-throated blue warblers in a dense understory of hobblebush.

  • This species: mature forest with a closed canopy, large trees and sparse ground cover (Audubon, as recorded in the note); it nests on the ground in leaf litter, so a large, unbroken block matters most.

Legal status

Native birds are covered by the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which prohibits taking (killing, capturing, selling, trading and transport are named) protected migratory birds without prior authorization (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service page, read through a summarizing fetch 2026-10-31). The Act applies to native species named in the treaties (50 CFR 10.13); that list was not checked for this species, and the page read says nothing about nests or about cutting trees in nesting season. Ask the county forester or the Fish and Wildlife Service before work near an active nest.
