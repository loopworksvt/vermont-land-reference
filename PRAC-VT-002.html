Silvicultural systems and treatments

practicesilviculture

Silviculture is how a forester tends and renews a stand of trees. A system is a way of regenerating and harvesting it. Vermont's Use Value Appraisal plans name the system for each stand, so these are the terms an owner will meet. This note describes what the sources say; it does not say which fits a parcel.

Topic
silviculture
Who is involved
landowner, consulting forester, county forester
Related rules
R-VT-001, R-VT-006, T-VT-001, S-VT-006

When it fits

Decided stand by stand by a forester from the inventory, the owner's goals, the species and the site, and (for enrolled land) the plan's enrollment subcategory. In the UVA Standards a stand is managed as even-aged, two-aged or uneven-aged (the plan states which, at a minimum).

What the Standards and the Guides say about the main ones:

  • Intermediate treatments and tending: total basal area is not reduced below the "B" level of the stocking guide, no single entry reduces stocking by more than one-third of basal area, and acceptable growing stock stays above the "C" level (or 60 square feet per acre if no guide applies). Residual trees should be healthy, vigorous and sound.
  • Crop tree release: cutting competing trees around the best trees so they grow faster (Forestry Glossary); how to choose the trees, how many sides to free and what to cut are in Crop Tree Release.
  • Clearcut and progressive clearcut: removing all trees larger than 2 inches DBH in a stand or part of one.
  • Shelterwood: large, vigorous, well-formed trees of desirable species are kept until the final cutting; the key idea is that a new crop is established before the old one is finished. Overstory removal takes the overstory off once the next stand of acceptable species is established.
  • Seed tree cut: an even-aged regeneration cut that leaves seed trees; the plan gives the number of crop or seed trees per acre.
  • Uneven-aged management (single tree selection, group selection, irregular shelterwood types): the ratio of acceptable growing stock to total basal area is maintained or increased; between groups, total basal area is not reduced below the "B" line. The plan states diameter objectives for the principal species and the cutting cycle (years between regeneration treatments).
  • Group selection size in deer yards: 0.25 to 2 acres, with stand entry every 10 to 20 years and at least 50 percent functional cover kept at all times (Landowner's Guide, deer chapter).
  • Shelterwood versus clearcut in deer yards: shelterwood is the more reliable regeneration method; clearcutting is the least preferred. In a two-stage shelterwood the first cut is in late summer (to prepare a seedbed for spruce and reduce damage to the residual stand) and the second in winter, protecting regeneration that has reached 6 inches to 3 feet.
  • Regeneration must succeed: a regeneration treatment that takes stocking below the "C" line must establish at least 350 stems per acre of acceptable commercial or native species within ten years (UVA Standards 2026 §3.2.3); if it does not, the next plan must prescribe work to meet it. (An older 2014 manual summary gave five years; the 2026 text controls.)

How the systems compare in northern hardwoods (search summaries of the USDA Forest Service northern hardwood guide and a UVM review, not read in full): single-tree selection suits stands that already have enough quality trees, gives the most flexibility and an unbroken look, but favors shade-tolerant species (beech and sugar maple) and may not stimulate regeneration; group selection keeps an uneven-aged structure and recruits mid-tolerant as well as tolerant species, but the mosaic of even-aged groups can complicate later management; shelterwood is effective when the number of cuts, timing and overstory density fit the stand; beech and deer often decide whether the desired species regenerate.

Who does what

The consulting forester writes the prescription and marks the trees; the county forester approves it as part of the plan; the logger carries it out; the owner decides the goals and signs off. See Forest Management and Harvesting Overview.

Timing

Winter and summer are the usual logging seasons (Harvest Timing and Seasons). Clearcutting aspen for sprouting is done during dormancy (fall, once the leaves are off, or winter) per the grouse guidance (Ruffed Grouse, Quaking Aspen). In enrolled land prescribed treatments may happen within three years either side of the plan year (S-VT-006 Activity Window).

Costs and benefits

The sources read give no cost comparison. Qualitatively: selection and group selection keep cover (useful for wildlife and winter deer cover) and spread income over time but need repeat entries; shelterwood and clearcut renew the stand reliably but change the look and the habitat sharply for years; thinning and crop tree release cost money up front (precommercial work yields no sale) and pay back in growth on the best trees. See Harvest Costs Income and Incentives.

Risks and what to avoid

  • A treatment that is not in the approved plan or is outside its window (T-VT-001 Cutting Contrary to the Plan).
  • Cutting below the stocking floors above.
  • Heavy cuts of 40 acres or more need an intent-to-cut notice (Legal Checks Before a Harvest).
  • Clearcutting a high-value beech or oak (mast) stand for wildlife (Ruffed Grouse).
  • Regeneration failure from beech sprouting, invasive plants or over-browsing by deer and moose (Harvest Close-out and Follow-up).

Rules that apply

UVA Minimum Management and Plan Standards 2026 (§3 on harvest parameters, §4 on plan elements); R-VT-006 Reserve Forestland for stands managed for old-forest characteristics (no cutting cycle).

Not recorded here

Which system is best for a given stand type, stocking guides and the numbers behind the B and C lines, diameter-limit cutting (a practice foresters caution against; not read), and any cost per acre.
