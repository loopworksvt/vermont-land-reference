Names

Bufflehead (Bucephala albeola).

Range and habitat

Spring and fall migrant on the Champlain Valley checklist (spring and fall only). On migration and in winter: lakes, ponds and rivers, and sheltered bays and estuaries (Audubon). Its nesting habitat is ponds and small lakes in open mixed forest, burned areas and aspen groves, in northern Canada (Audubon).

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

13 to 15 inches, the smallest of the diving ducks here. The breeding male has a white 'scarf' on a round black head and a white body with a black back; the female has a gray head with a white ear spot. The male gives a squeaky whistle; the female a soft, hoarse quack (Audubon).

Signs on the land (tracks, scat, browse, nests, holes, calls)

A very small black-and-white duck on open water; the sources read name no other sign.

Role in the forest (what it eats, what eats it, what it needs)

Mainly aquatic insects in fresh water, crustaceans on the ocean, snails and other mollusks in winter (Audubon). Nests in tree cavities, especially old Northern Flicker holes, usually 2 to 10 feet up; 8 to 10 eggs incubated 29 to 31 days (Audubon). Overland migration is in small flocks, mostly at night.

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

Open lakes and ponds with insects and snails on migration; for breeding, woodland ponds with old woodpecker holes (not a Vermont record). Audubon says the species is much less numerous than historically because of unrestricted shooting and nesting-habitat loss, and is still fairly common and apparently stable. The sources read give no Vermont advice.

Managing for it (habitat practices) and living with it (conflicts and damage)

Nothing recorded for private land. Ducks, geese and mergansers have hunting seasons set each year by Vermont Fish & Wildlife in three zones (Lake Champlain, Interior Vermont and Connecticut River). The 2024-25 dates and bag limits are in the Department's 2024 Waterfowl Program Legislative Report (read as text 2026-11-01); they change each year, so check the current Vermont hunting regulations before acting on them. See Waterfowl and Migration in Vermont for the Vermont season table, where to watch, and what helps waterfowl on your land.

Legal status

Native birds are covered by the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which prohibits taking (killing, capturing, selling, trading and transport are named) protected migratory birds without prior authorization (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service page, read through a summarizing fetch 2026-10-31). The Act applies to native species named in the treaties (50 CFR 10.13); that list was not checked for this species.
