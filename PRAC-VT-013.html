Preventing tick and biting fly bites in the woods

practicehealth-and-safety

What a person working or walking in Vermont woods and fields can do so that ticks do not get on them, and what the sources say about deer flies, horse flies, black flies and mosquitoes. The steps for ticks are the same advice from the Vermont Department of Health and the CDC: protect, check, remove, watch. For the biting flies the sources are weaker: the advice differs by source and by fly, and the sources say so.

Topic
health-and-safety
Who is involved
landowner, forester, logger, hiker

When it fits

Every time you go into woods, brush or tall grass in Vermont. The Health Department says ticks are common throughout the state whenever temperatures are above freezing; the blacklegged tick's nymphs peak in May and June and a second rise of adults comes in October and November (Blacklegged Tick). Fieldwork such as marking trees, cruising timber, trail work or hunting puts a person in the habitat for hours, so it applies to a forester, a logger and a landowner alike.

Ticks: protect, check, remove, watch

Before and during: protect (Vermont Department of Health, CDC)

  • Choose the route. Avoid wooded and brushy areas with tall grass, shrubs and leaf litter when you can. Walk in the center of trails and avoid brushing against tall grass and vegetation.
  • Dress for it. Wear long pants, long-sleeved shirts, long socks and boots; light-colored clothing makes ticks easier to spot. See below).
  • Treat clothes and gear with permethrin. Use a product with 0.5 percent permethrin on boots, clothing and camping gear, or buy pre-treated items; the CDC says protection lasts through several washings. Never put permethrin on skin; it is for clothing and gear only.
  • Use an EPA-registered repellent on skin with one of these active ingredients: DEET, picaridin, IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE), para-menthane-diol (PMD) or 2-undecanone; check the label for an EPA registration number and follow it. Do not use OLE or PMD products on children under 3. The CDC says to put on sunscreen first, then repellent.
  • Pets: ask a veterinarian about oral, topical or collar tick prevention and check pets after time outdoors.

After you come in: check

  • Check clothing, gear and pets before going indoors. Ticks can ride in on a coat, a daypack or a dog and attach later.
  • Do a full-body check on yourself and any children, with a handheld or full-length mirror. Look closely at the underarms, in and around the ears, inside the belly button, behind the knees, in and around the hair and hairline, between the legs and around the waist.
  • Shower within 2 hours of coming indoors; it washes off unattached ticks (CDC says it lowers the risk of Lyme disease).
  • Put the clothes in a dryer on high heat for at least 10 minutes to kill ticks (damp clothes need longer). If the clothes must be washed first, use hot water: cold or warm water will not kill ticks.

If one is attached: remove (Vermont Department of Health)

  1. Use clean, fine-tipped tweezers or a tick removal tool to grasp the tick as close to the skin's surface as possible.
  2. Pull straight up with steady, even pressure. Do not twist or jerk; that can break off the mouthparts. If mouthparts stay in the skin, try to remove them with tweezers; if they will not come out easily, leave them and let the skin heal.
  3. Clean the bite area and your hands with soap and water or rubbing alcohol.
  4. Dispose of the tick: put it in alcohol, seal it in a bag or container, wrap it tightly in tape, or flush it. Never crush it with your fingers.

Most ticks can be removed at home without a health care visit. The Health Department does not recommend testing a removed tick (a positive result does not confirm infection, a negative result can falsely reassure, and results can delay treatment); it and the Agency of Agriculture do not test ticks submitted by the public, though the Agency's survey program accepts ticks for identification.

Afterward: watch

Watch for symptoms for 30 days after a bite. The Health Department names fever, chills, rash, headache, joint pain, muscle aches and fatigue. A Lyme rash usually starts near the bite and gradually expands and may look like a bull's-eye, but not always; the other Vermont tick-bite illnesses typically do not cause a rash. If symptoms appear, call a health care provider and say when the bite happened and that you may have been exposed to a tick (Blacklegged Tick for the illnesses).

On your own land: make a tick-safer yard (CDC and the Connecticut handbook)

The aim is to make the area you use less welcoming to ticks, not to treat the whole property. The CDC and the Tick Management Handbook both say: keep grass mowed; remove leaf litter and clear tall grass and brush at the lawn edge and around the house; put a barrier 3 feet wide or more of wood chips, mulch or gravel between lawn and woods or stone walls; stack wood neatly in a dry place; keep playground equipment, decks and patios away from yard edges and trees; use fences to keep out deer and other animals. The Handbook adds: trim tree branches and shrubs at the lawn edge to let in more sunlight, because sun and lower humidity make the area less hospitable to ticks; keep firewood piles and bird feeders away from the house; and keep mice out of stone walls and wood piles. The CDC says not to rely on spraying; check with local health or agricultural officials about timing and product. The handbook's tick counts are from Connecticut yards.

Deer flies and horse flies

Both belong to one family and only the females bite (Deer Flies, Horse Flies). What the sources say about protection:

  • They are drawn to dark moving objects and to carbon dioxide (University of Wisconsin), they circle and follow a person until they get a bite, and a person is easiest to find while moving. Vermont Public's article, quoting entomologist Judy Rosovsky, says biting flies are attracted to blue (it mentions walking with a companion wearing blue), and the only other advice it gives is to stay indoors, swat, or whirl something around your head.
  • Repellents: the sources disagree. The Vermont Public article says the jury is out on whether bug sprays, including DEET, deter these flies. The University of Wisconsin sheet says standard repellents are only marginally effective and that products with high levels of DEET give some protection.
  • Spraying the yard or the animal does not work (University of Wisconsin): adults do not rest on a surface long enough for a residual spray to work, and fogging only kills the flies present at the time; flies can return in minutes. A dark, ball-shaped trap hung in the wind (the Manning trap) works on the same attraction to dark moving objects; the sheet shows a diagram.
  • Pets and livestock: provide a shed or doghouse for shelter; insecticide sprays on animals are ineffective because the flies land for so short a time.
  • When: the UW sheet says adults can be active from May to September, with deer flies commonly biting in June and July; the Vermont Public article puts the deer fly peak around the end of July and says a wet spring followed by heat made 2025 a very bad year.
  • Health: the Vermont Public article reports painful bites that can swell and itch for days and quotes the entomologist that being annoying is not a public health threat. The UW sheet says some people have fever, sores or allergic responses, and that diseases are mechanically transmitted elsewhere in the world but have not been a major problem in Wisconsin.

Black flies

Only the female bites; they breed in running water, most abundantly at the outlets of ponds and lakes, and large numbers suggest clean streams (UNH Extension). In New Hampshire two species consistently bite people: Simulium venustum emerges in early to mid-May in the south and stays a pest to the end of May, and in the north it emerges in late May or early June and can stay abundant until the end of June, or into July in the mountains (Black Flies). The sheet's protection advice (written for New Hampshire):

  • Clothing: short sleeves with a closed front (zippered shirts keep flies out better than button shirts) and trousers tucked into socks or high boots. Colors: light shades such as orange, yellow and light blue attract fewer black flies than dark green, brown and red.
  • Head net: a shoulder-length net is sometimes useful and can be treated with repellent.
  • Repellent: mosquito repellents work but may not last as long against black flies; apply at the wrists, ankles and belt line. DEET is the most effective ingredient; picaridin and IR3535 are nearly as effective.
  • When: flies are active only by day, with peaks around 9 to 11 AM and 4 to 7 PM, highest on humid, cloudy days and before storms; the sheet says early morning, midday and late evening are the best times to work outdoors.
  • Control: individuals should not try to control larvae; treating a small area does not help, and surface-water treatments need a state permit rarely given to individuals. Fogging only gives hours of relief.

Mosquitoes

The Health Department's mosquito pages track West Nile virus, eastern equine encephalitis and Jamestown Canyon virus in mosquitoes collected in Vermont, and say the share of positive samples historically rises in late August and peaks in mid-September. For eastern equine encephalitis the state also recommends permethrin on clothing and gear (Mosquitoes).

Timing

Season by season, from the sources above: ticks whenever it is above freezing, with blacklegged nymphs peaking in May and June and adults rising again in October and November; black flies from early May into June (later and longer at elevation, from the New Hampshire dates), by day, peaking about 9 to 11 AM and 4 to 7 PM; deer flies commonly in June and July with a peak around the end of July in a wet-then-hot year; horse flies and deer flies from May to September in the Wisconsin sheet. These are not Vermont records for the flies.

Who does what

The owner or worker does the protecting and checking. A health care provider decides on any illness after a bite.

Costs and benefits

The sources give no prices. Permethrin-treated clothing or a spray to treat your own, repellents and a head net are the costs; the benefit is fewer bites and, for ticks, a lower chance of tick-bite illness (CDC says showering within two hours lowers the risk of Lyme disease).

Risks and what to avoid

Crushing a tick with your fingers; twisting or jerking it; putting permethrin on skin; using OLE or PMD products on children under 3; relying on spraying the yard or the woods; assuming a repellent that works on ticks will work on deer flies; going without a check because it was only a short walk; leaving tick-carrying clothes in a hamper or on a chair without drying them on high heat.

Rules that apply

Pesticide applications to surface water in New Hampshire require a permit (UNH).
