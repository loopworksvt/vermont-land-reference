Names

Turkey tail, Trametes versicolor. Family Polyporaceae.

Thin, flexible, banded shelf fungus found in overlapping rosettes on dead hardwood logs and stumps all year. It is among the commonest wood fungi in North America.

Range and habitat

Saprobic on hardwood deadwood and rarely on conifer wood, causing white rot of the sapwood; grows in overlapping clusters on logs and stumps; very widely distributed and common in North America (MushroomExpert.com, Kuo).

How to identify

  • Cap 2 to 8 cm across, only 1 to 2 mm thick, flexible when fresh, densely hairy or velvety, with concentric zones of different colors.
  • Underside whitish to pale brown, with pores (3 to 6 or more per millimeter) and not gills.
  • Flesh whitish, tough and leathery; spore print whitish.
  • The page offers a "turkey tail test" to separate the real species from look-alikes: look at the zoning, the flexibility and the pores.

Look-alikes

  • Crust fungi such as Stereum ostrea (false turkey tail): no true pores.
  • Trametes hirsuta (whitish to grayish fresh cap), T. pubescens (no starkly contrasting zones), T. ochracea (rigid and hard, not thin and flexible).
  • Other Trametes have 1 to 3 pores per millimeter.

Through the year

Year-round (MushroomExpert.com).

What the sources say about eating and poison

Not a food. MushroomExpert.com gives no edibility information, and says the mushroom's medicinal reputation does not hold up; it does not recommend it as a cure.

Never eat a wild mushroom because of anything on this page. See Mushrooms in Vermont Woods and call a poison center (1-800-222-1222) if someone has eaten one.

What helps it thrive

It feeds on dead hardwood. Logs, stumps and fallen limbs left in the woods give it a place; the sources read say nothing else.
