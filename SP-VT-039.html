Names

Quackgrass, Elymus repens, a grass in the family Poaceae. Go Botany calls it creeping wild-rye.

Range and habitat

Not native. Go Botany describes it as a Eurasian grass, invasive and present throughout New England, in disturbed or maintained places, meadows and fields, and occasionally on cobble river shores. The fire-effects page calls it exotic and says it invades "gardens, yards, crop fields, roadsides, ditches, and just about any disturbed, moist area", is widespread in eastern Canada, and is absent from the Gulf Coast states.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Growth habit (UVM): a tall, creeping grass with extensive rhizomes that forms an open sod, growing best in loose soils. UVM says it is typically considered a weed. Leaves are rolled in the bud shoot.
  • Auricles: long, clasping auricles at the base of the blade (UVM); this is the feature to look for. Sheaths can be hairy but not always.
  • Ligule: medium-long and blunt (UVM); 0.25 to 1.5 mm (Go Botany).
  • Seedhead: an unbranched spike 50 to 150 mm long (Go Botany) with the spikelets set flat against the stem, 1 to 2 per node and without stalks; each spikelet has 4 to 7 florets.
  • Leaf: blades 6 to 10 mm wide, 6 to 30 cm long.
  • Height: 50 to 100 cm.
  • Look-alikes: none listed in the sources read. Among the grasses in the UVM table, quackgrass is the only one whose entry lists long, clasping auricles (see How to Identify Grasses).

Role in the field

The fire-effects page calls it an undesirable weed species and an early dominant on disturbed ground. In a Minnesota prairie it continued to spread into adjacent areas after five annual burns.

What helps it thrive (light, soil and moisture, and how it is kept)

  • Soil and water: the review gives no light, pH or fertility needs; it invades gardens, yards, crop fields, roadsides, ditches and almost any disturbed, moist area (FEIS).
  • Spread: mainly by rhizomes that can grow 23 inches or more from the main shoot, and also by seed. Not something to encourage in a field you want to keep for other plants.
  • Control: mowing and raking significantly reduced quackgrass biomass in Midwestern prairies; late spring fires generally reduce cover while early spring fires can increase it.
  • Use to others: cover for small rodents, birds and waterfowl.

Managing for it and living with it

The fire-effects page, citing a Wisconsin study in prairie, reports that mowing and raking significantly reduced quackgrass biomass and prevented flowering the following year, and says mowing, burning and chemical treatment combined may be the best way to eradicate it. Those are prairie results; no Vermont advice was read.

Legal status
