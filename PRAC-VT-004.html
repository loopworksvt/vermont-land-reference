Selling timber and working with professionals

practiceselling

The usual sequence for a timber sale, the roles, the ways of being paid and what a contract should say.

Topic
selling
Who is involved
landowner, consulting forester, county forester, logger, buyer
Related rules
T-VT-001, S-VT-003

When it fits

When the owner decides to harvest for income or as part of a management plan. Start with a plan and a forester rather than a buyer; FPR's own advice is to be a cautious consumer when someone approaches with an unsolicited offer, to check their reputation and past jobs, and to have a forester or an experienced landowner read any contract offered.

How it is done

  1. Plan and forester. A forester inventories the forest, appraises its value and recommends a harvest.
  2. Find a forester. Ask the county forester for a list of consulting foresters (the county forester will not recommend one, but may direct you to landowners who will). Other leads: the Society of American Foresters (New England Chapter), the Vermont Woodlands Association's listing of certified Consulting Foresters, and other landowners. Ask for sample plans, maps and contracts; ask about their business practices for contracts, billing and schedules; get the cost of services in writing; check references.
  3. How the sale is structured. Competitive bid, negotiated price, or contract logging. The forester sets this up, may invite bids, and says how the volume will be measured and by whom. Know the units: saw timber is sold by the thousand board feet (MBF), pulpwood by the cord or ton, chips by the green ton.
  4. Choose the buyer or logger. A logging contractor buys your standing trees, cuts them, takes them to a landing and trucks them to market; you are the seller and they are the buyer, not employer and employee. Visit past jobs, ask for the last three landowners worked for, and ask the questions FPR lists (did they meet obligations, finish on time, communicate, avoid damage to trees, roads, fences and bridges, leave the site in good condition, would you hire them again). Vermont law does not require loggers to be licensed, certified or registered; voluntary programs include Vermont LEAP (Logger Education to Advance Professionalism) and Master Logger. Sometimes one person is both forester and logger, or a forester works with a buyer: in that case make sure your interests are protected and call the county forester.
  5. The written contract. See the list below. Review it on site with the forester and logger before signing; consider an attorney because a timber sale can be a significant transaction. A contract is not required by law, but if either side has to prove an agreement, a written contract is the only evidence an authority or court will accept (search summary of FPR guidance).
  6. Insurance. Get certificates before equipment arrives (details below).
  7. Harvest administration. The forester visits the site regularly through the harvest and close-out; administration ends when the last payment is made, the bond is returned and the contract conditions, including close-out, are met (Harvest Close-out and Follow-up).

Two ways to be paid for standing timber (FPR economics brochure):

  • Lump sum: an agreed price for all the trees harvested, based on an estimate of their value.
  • Mill scale: paid for each tree cut, measured and accounted at the mill.

A consulting forester helps decide which suits the harvest. Selling logs yourself, rather than standing timber, can add income but brings added risk and responsibility that many owners decide is not worth it.

A harvest contract should include (Guidelines 1.7 and the economics brochure): names and addresses of the seller, landowner and buyer or logger; the period covered and the signing date; a map of the parcel and the harvest area showing roads, streams and areas needing protection; the estimated volume by species and product, and the number of trees marked; a price breakdown; a payment schedule (the payments and due dates are often based on the percentage of acres or volume harvested); a cash bond, bank draft or letter of credit from the logger for the duration, returned on successful completion (usable to repair damage); general operating conditions (seasons, time of day); special conditions for protected areas; a clause making the logger responsible for state and federal rules, including the water-quality practices (the landowner is still ultimately responsible); the ability to suspend operations in bad conditions; and close-out requirements completed before the logger moves equipment off site.

Insurance (Guidelines 1.7): workers' compensation (protects the contractor if hurt), general liability naming the landowner as an additional insured, and automobile and equipment coverage. Ask for certificates showing the policy cannot be canceled mid-harvest without 30 days' notice by certified mail to the landowner. A landowner could be held liable for an accident on the property during a contracted harvest.

Timing

Decide the season in the contract; weather can halt a harvest (Harvest Timing and Seasons). Plan enough time for the plan, any amendment and the legal checks before the contract is signed (Legal Checks Before a Harvest).

Costs and benefits

Foresters work for a fee, which FPR calls usually well worth it. The Vermont Woodlands Association lists three common arrangements (flat fee for services such as plans, hourly rate, or a percentage of harvest revenue) per a search summary; neither FPR page gives a percentage or a dollar figure. Competitive bidding is said to raise prices. Timber income is generally treated as a long-term capital gain when the sale transfers an interest in the timber (lump sum or mill scale), but this depends on contract structure; ask a tax professional (search summary of an FPR page; see also www.timbertax.org, which the Guidelines cite). See Harvest Costs Income and Incentives.

Risks and what to avoid

Unsolicited offers from unknown buyers; an unreviewed contract; no bond or no insurance certificate; no close-out clause; unmarked harvest boundaries (timber trespass); handing the sale to someone who is both your forester and the buyer without independent advice.

Rules that apply

Legal Checks Before a Harvest, T-VT-001 Cutting Contrary to the Plan (enrolled land), conservation easements and certification agreements (the contract must comply).
