Wildlife habitat in harvest planning

practicehabitat

What to leave, protect or time around when cutting, so a harvest can serve both income and wildlife. It collects what the species notes say as a checklist for the planning stage.

Topic
habitat
Who is involved
landowner, consulting forester, logger
Related rules
R-VT-003, R-VT-004, R-VT-006

When it fits

Whenever a harvest is planned; the forester can build these into the marking and the contract at little or no cost.

How it is done

FeatureWhat to do (source)Species notes
Snags and den treesManage for at least six cavity, snag and/or decadent living trees per acre on average, with one over 18 inches DBH and three over 16 inches; prefer hardwoods with cavities high in the trunk; leave two or more large den trees per acre within 300 feet of ponds and lakes; keep six or more snags of 15 inches or larger within 300 feet of openings and water; cluster them away from roads and trails for safety; if one must be felled, leave it on the ground. Where snags are scarce, girdle or leave unhealthy trees. (Landowner's Guide)Pileated Woodpecker, Barred Owl, Fisher
Down woody material and slashLeave coarse woody debris and slash on the forest floor; four downed trees per acre is a bird-habitat figure; slash helps ground-nesting birds, amphibians and insects.Wood Frog, Ruffed Grouse
Deer winter cover (yards)Keep mature softwood with 70 to 90 percent winter canopy closure and at least 50 percent functional cover at all times; use group selection of 0.25 to 2 acres, entries every 10 to 20 years; shelterwood rather than clearcut; keep hemlock stands; keep travel lanes 200 feet wide; no dogs running loose in yards. The Wetland Rules also require following the deer-yard silvicultural standards in mapped yards.White-tailed Deer, Eastern Hemlock
Vernal poolsNo activity within the pool depression and adjacent 100 feet; no permanent roads or development 100 to 650 feet; keep 60 percent canopy; harvest on frozen, snow-covered ground; leave downed wood; avoid deep ruts.Wood Frog
Raptor nestsBuffers at least the height of the tallest tree within 20 feet of the nest; no harvest in the buffer April through June; no large openings within 300 feet; leave wildlife trees of 12 inches DBH or more, one or more per acre.Barred Owl
Mast trees (beech, oak, hickory, cherry, apple)Keep a mix; release oak crowns (can raise acorn production several times); be cautious logging at the edge of beech and oak stands with bear sign; do not clearcut a key mast area for grouse; keep healthy beech in all ages.Black Bear, Wild Turkey, American Beech, Northern Red Oak
Young forest and brushSmall clearcuts of one to several acres give berries, cherries and browse; aspen clearcut in dormancy sprouts for grouse; moose want 10 to 20 percent of their range in regeneration under 20 years old, in cuts no larger than 10 acres (square) or under 200 yards wide.Ruffed Grouse, Moose, Snowshoe Hare, Quaking Aspen
Softwood cover for hares and mooseKeep dense conifer base and travel cover; moose want winter shelter on 5 to 15 percent of range in stands of at least 10 acres with trees at least 35 feet tall.Snowshoe Hare, Moose
Forested wetlands and streamsKeep a minimum 100-foot undisturbed buffer around forested wetlands for bears; keep streams shaded and sediment-free with vegetated buffers; leave some downed trees along water for turtle basking.Black Bear, Wood Frog
Openings and edgesSeed log landings and woods roads with grass and legume mixes, mow periodically, keep 5 to 10 percent of an area in small herbaceous openings; reclaim brushy areas outside nesting season.Wild Turkey, Ruffed Grouse, Snowshoe Hare
Invasive plantsFind and remove before the harvest so they do not take over regeneration.Buckthorns, Japanese Barberry, Garlic Mustard

Who does what

The forester marks retention trees, maps the features and writes them into the contract; the logger works around them; the owner decides the balance between income and habitat. For habitat questions, ask the Fish & Wildlife district biologist.

Timing

See Harvest Timing and Seasons for nesting and winter-cover windows.

Costs and benefits

Retention trees and set-asides forgo some timber value (not quantified in the sources read); they cost little to mark. Benefits are habitat for many species and, in enrolled land, a better fit to plan elements and to Reserve or Ecologically Significant Treatment Area subcategories (R-VT-006 Reserve Forestland). Combining deer habitat work with work for other species of conservation concern is encouraged.

Risks and what to avoid

Cutting den trees without a plan for safety; roads through buffers; clearcutting mast stands; harvesting in nesting windows.

Rules that apply

AMPs and the Wetland Rules are law (Legal Checks Before a Harvest); the rest is recommended practice. Rare, threatened and endangered species are protected by statute.

Not recorded here

Bat roost and maternity windows, the Fish & Wildlife review process for chip harvesting, and chapter 5 of the Guidelines in detail.
