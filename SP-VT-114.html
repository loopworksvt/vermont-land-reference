Names

Ring-necked Duck (Aythya collaris).

Range and habitat

Rare breeder in Vermont: confirmed nesting only in a handful of blocks, mostly in the Northern Vermont Piedmont and Northeastern Highlands, and once in the Southern Green Mountains (Atlas). Nesting was suggested as early as 1949 and first confirmed in Vermont in 1965. A migrant elsewhere in the state: the Champlain Valley checklist lists it in spring, summer and fall. Habitat: wooded lakes and ponds; in summer freshwater marshes, ponds and bogs, mainly in forest openings (Audubon); the Atlas says it nests over water in nutrient-poor bogs and other stable wetlands.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

15 to 18 inches. The breeding male has a black head and back with a white bar separating gray sides from a black chest; the female is mostly gray-brown with a pale area near the base of the bill. The ring on the bill is distinct; the neck ring that gives the species its name is very hard to see. Usually silent (Audubon).

The Atlas recorded females with young between 16 June and 23 July.

Role in the forest (what it eats, what eats it, what it needs)

Mostly aquatic plant seeds, stems and roots (pondweeds, sedges, smartweeds, grasses), plus aquatic insects and mollusks; ducklings eat mainly insects (Audubon). The nest is a shallow bowl of grasses and sedges on a dry hummock, brush clump or floating mat close to open water; 8 to 10 eggs incubated 25 to 29 days by the female (Audubon). Migrates in flocks, late in fall and early in spring (Audubon).

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

Stable water levels and abundant emergent, submersed or floating aquatic plants in nutrient-poor bogs and other stable wetlands, with dense herbaceous vegetation to hold the nest over water (Atlas, citing Hohman and Eberhardt 1998). The Atlas says the Vermont range expansion paralleled a continental population increase and that no current management is recommended. Audubon says it has become much more widespread as a breeding bird in eastern Canada and northern New England since the 1930s.

Managing for it (habitat practices) and living with it (conflicts and damage)

No management is recommended (Atlas). Keep bogs and stable wetlands undisturbed and keep water levels stable. Observe from a distance. Ducks, geese and mergansers have hunting seasons set each year by Vermont Fish & Wildlife in three zones (Lake Champlain, Interior Vermont and Connecticut River). The 2024-25 dates and bag limits are in the Department's 2024 Waterfowl Program Legislative Report; they change each year, so check the current Vermont hunting regulations before acting on them. See Waterfowl and Migration in Vermont for the Vermont season table, where to watch, and what helps waterfowl on your land.

Legal status

Native birds are covered by the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which prohibits taking (killing, capturing, selling, trading and transport are named) protected migratory birds without prior authorization (U.S. The Act applies to native species named in the treaties (50 CFR 10.13).
