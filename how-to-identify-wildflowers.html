How to identify wildflowers (a field guide to the common ones, by color and shape)

guideidentifying the wildflowers of a Vermont field and open area, by flower color and shape

How to use this guide, and what it does not cover

Look at three things in this order: the color of the flower, its shape (a daisy-like head, a round cluster, a spike, a bell), and how the leaves are attached to the stem. Each answer points to a few plants of the 19 that have their own page in this library; open the page for the full description, the look-alikes and the photograph.

Three cautions. Color is a starting point, not an answer: Go Botany lists a range of colors for several of these plants (for example "blue to purple, pink to red, or white" for Queen Anne's lace), and the photographs show one plant on one day. The guide covers only these 19 plants. It does not cover the shrubs and wildflowers of woods and wet ground, and a plant that fits none of the keys is simply not in the library yet. Nothing here says a plant is safe to touch or eat; where a note gives no look-alike comparison it says so, and for white umbrella-shaped flowers it tells you to ask (Queen Anne's Lace).

This is general reference, not a plant list for any particular land. A forester or botanist should name anything that matters to a plan.

Quick key from the flower color

Yellow

  • Daisy-like head with a dark center and yellow rays, and toothed leaves that are hairy underneath: Black-eyed Susan.
  • Many small yellow heads on separate stalks that tend to arch to one side of the stem; leaves with three main veins: Canada Goldenrod.
  • Yellow heads of rays only on a stalk, with all the leaves in a ground-level rosette and lobed: Common Dandelion.
  • One to seven yellow pea-like flowers on a stalk, over leaves of five small leaflets: Birdsfoot Trefoil.
  • Five open yellow petals with black dots along the edges, small opposite leaves: Common St. Johnswort.
  • Orange and yellow, snapdragon-like, with a spur: Butter-and-eggs.

Orange

  • Orange heads of rays only, leaves untoothed at the base and on the stem, some plants spreading by stolons: Orange Hawkweed.
  • Orange and yellow two-lipped flower with a spur: Butter-and-eggs.

White

  • White rays around a flat yellow center, one to three heads on a stem: Oxeye Daisy.
  • A flat-topped cluster of small white heads over feathery, many-lobed leaves: Common Yarrow.
  • A flat or dome-topped umbel of tiny flowers over very finely cut compound leaves: Queen Anne's Lace.

Pink, red and purple

  • A rounded cluster of pink to purplish flowers at the upper joints of a tall plant with big, opposite, oblong leaves: Common Milkweed.
  • A round pink-red head above leaves of three leaflets marked with spots or stripes: Red Clover.
  • A long spike of four-petaled pink-to-purple flowers on a plant with narrow alternate leaves: Fireweed.
  • A flat-topped cluster of small blue-purple heads with no rays, over whorls of toothed leaves, in wet ground: Spotted Joe-Pye Weed.
  • Daisy-like heads with many narrow deep red-purple rays: New England Aster.
  • Round clusters of two-lipped, usually light purple flowers on a roughly square stem: Wild Bergamot.

Blue and blue-purple

  • Blue-purple heads of strap-shaped rays only, no disk, along roads and field edges: Chicory.
  • A nodding blue bell on a thin stem, with rounded basal leaves and narrow stem leaves: Harebell.
  • Slender upright spikes of small blue-purple flowers in wet fields: Blue Vervain.
  • (also, in the lists above, the blue-purple of Spotted Joe-Pye Weed, the violet of New England Aster and the light purple of Wild Bergamot)

When they bloom (approximate)

Read them as a rough order.

  • April or May on: Common Dandelion (April to September in the Minnesota source).
  • June on, through summer: Common Milkweed, Black-eyed Susan, Wild Bergamot, Blue Vervain, Common Yarrow, Fireweed, Harebell, Oxeye Daisy, Queen Anne's Lace, Red Clover, Birdsfoot Trefoil, Chicory, Butter-and-eggs, Orange Hawkweed, Common St. Johnswort.
  • July on, into fall: Canada Goldenrod, Spotted Joe-Pye Weed.
  • August on, into October: New England Aster.

Look-alikes the sources name

  • Fireweed and purple loosestrife. Go Botany says fireweed has four petals, alternate leaves and seeds with a tuft of white hairs; purple loosestrife has six petals and opposite or whorled leaves. Purple loosestrife is on the Agency's quarantine list (Tier 3) (Fireweed).
  • Common milkweed and purple milkweed. Go Botany separates them by the size and color of the hoods and by the pods (Common Milkweed).
  • Wild bergamot and purple bergamot. The purple one has a red-purple corolla and wider leaf blades (Wild Bergamot).
  • Common St. Johnswort and spotted St. Johnswort. The common one has black dots on the petals only near the edges (Common St. Johnswort).
  • Chicory and endive. Endive has purple rays and hairless leaves (Chicory).
  • Common yarrow and a related yarrow. Common yarrow has 15 or more leaf divisions that are not in one plane (Common Yarrow).
  • Do not use this guide to tell it from them; ask before handling or eating a plant (Queen Anne's Lace).

Rare relatives: some wildflowers are protected

But the list does include close relatives of some of them, so an unfamiliar milkweed or goldenrod may be protected: whorled milkweed (endangered), blunt-leaved milkweed and butterfly-weed (threatened); sweet goldenrod (threatened) and elm-leaved goldenrod (endangered); sessile-leaved boneset (endangered, in the genus of Joe-Pye weed's old name); purple giant hyssop and yellow giant hyssop (threatened, a different plant from wild bergamot); obedient plant (threatened); and wild garlic (threatened). The rule lists many more plants. Do not pick, dig or move a plant you cannot name; ask the county forester or the Vermont Fish & Wildlife Department's botanist.
