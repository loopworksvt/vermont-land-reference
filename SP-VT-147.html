Names

Eastern gray squirrel, Sciurus carolinensis.

The familiar gray squirrel of woods, parks and yards. It plants forests by forgetting nuts it has buried.

Range and habitat

Mature, dense woodland of oak, hickory and other nut trees, and also urban parks, yards and farmland, across eastern North America (Wikipedia).

How to identify

  • Head and body 23 to 30 cm, tail 19 to 25 cm; adults weigh 400 to 600 g.
  • Gray fur with brown spots and streaks, a white underside and a large bushy tail; black and white forms occur, often in towns.
  • The hind claws point backward, which lets it come down a trunk head first. Call: a raspy 'mehr mehr mehr'; 'kuk' or 'quaa' as an alarm.

Look-alikes

  • Fox squirrel is sometimes confused with it (Wikipedia).
  • Red squirrel: smaller and reddish.

Signs on the land

  • Bounding tracks with a stride of 60 to 90 cm.
  • Dreys: round leaf-and-twig nests 30 to 60 cm across in tree forks, lined with moss, grass or feathers; also tree hollows.
  • Bark torn off to reach the soft layer underneath.

Through the year

Active by day (and at dusk, by season); it does not hibernate. Breeds in winter and again in early summer; after about 44 days a litter of one to four is born. Young are weaned at about ten weeks and leave the nest after twelve.

Role on the land

Wikipedia calls it the most important natural forest regenerator in eastern North America, because it buries thousands of nuts and does not find them all. It eats seeds, nuts, buds, flowers, fungi, insects and eggs. In buildings it can gnaw wiring and nest in attics and walls.

What helps it thrive

Mature woodland with mast-producing trees: litter size drops in poor food years.
