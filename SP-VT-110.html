Names

Wood Duck (Aix sponsa).

Range and habitat

Summer breeder across Vermont wherever mature trees offer nesting cavities along the riparian corridors of streams and rivers, in woodlands beside beaver ponds, and in swamps and freshwater marshes (Atlas). Broods use farm ponds and lakes but generally slow waterways, swamps and marshes. Vermont is at the northeastern edge of the species' range. The Champlain Valley checklist lists it in spring, summer and fall.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

17 to 20 inches long. The drake is colorful and unmistakable much of the year: an iridescent green crested head with white lines, a red eye and bill with a yellow base, a chestnut chest spotted white. In eclipse plumage (late summer) the male looks like the female but has a more distinct white throat. The female has a dark back, a gray head with a white eye-patch and a hint of a crest; in flight both look long-tailed and dark. The hen's call is a loud 'wooo-eeek!' (Audubon).

Signs on the land (tracks, scat, browse, nests, holes, calls)

Hens with broods on farm ponds, slow waterways, swamps and marshes, which was the most frequent source of breeding records in the Atlas; large tree cavities near water (Audubon says nest cavities can be up to 65 feet high).

Role in the forest (what it eats, what eats it, what it needs)

Mostly seeds, including those of aquatic plants and fallen tree and shrub seeds; acorns are a major food in many areas; also insects, crustaceans and waste grain; young birds eat mainly insects (Audubon). Wood ducks do not excavate cavities but use natural wood rot (Atlas); large tree cavities up to 65 feet high, lined with down, and nest boxes (Audubon). Clutches of 10 to 14 eggs (more if several females share a nest), incubated 28 to 37 days; the ducklings jump from the cavity the morning after hatching and fly at about 8 to 9 weeks. One brood a year in the north (Atlas, Audubon). Northern birds migrate; pairs form on the wintering grounds (Audubon).

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

Mature trees with natural cavities close to water (wood rot cavities, mostly in older trees); slow water, beaver ponds, swamps and marshes with shallow water; acorns and other seeds. The Atlas credits the 87-percent increase to maturing forests, protection of wetlands from pollutants and siltation, and expanding beaver populations, and says the Wood Duck's future in the Northeast looks secure if forests continue to mature and are managed on longer rotations to grow sawlogs, if beaver populations expand, and if wetlands are managed and protected from pollutants and siltation. Nest boxes: Audubon credits legal protection and nest boxes with the recovery of the species and says boxes are readily used, even low and in open marsh; the Atlas says boxes may produce only about 4 to 5 percent of the eastern population but may raise local densities in marshes that lack suitable nest trees, and makes no nest-box recommendation.

Managing for it (habitat practices) and living with it (conflicts and damage)

Keep large old trees, especially dead or rotting ones with cavities, along streams, beaver ponds and wetlands, and keep wetlands free of pollutants and siltation (Atlas). Harvesting near wetlands is covered in Wildlife Habitat in Harvest Planning. Ducks, geese and mergansers have hunting seasons set each year by Vermont Fish & Wildlife in three zones (Lake Champlain, Interior Vermont and Connecticut River). The 2024-25 dates and bag limits are in the Department's 2024 Waterfowl Program Legislative Report (read as text 2026-11-01); they change each year, so check the current Vermont hunting regulations before acting on them. The 2024-25 daily limit of 6 ducks could include no more than 3 wood ducks. See Waterfowl and Migration in Vermont for the Vermont season table, where to watch, and what helps waterfowl on your land.

Legal status

Native birds are covered by the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which prohibits taking (killing, capturing, selling, trading and transport are named) protected migratory birds without prior authorization (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service page, read through a summarizing fetch 2026-10-31). The Act applies to native species named in the treaties (50 CFR 10.13); that list was not checked for this species.
