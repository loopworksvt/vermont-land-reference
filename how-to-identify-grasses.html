How to identify grasses (a field guide to the common ones)

guideidentifying the common grasses of a Vermont open field

Why grasses are hard, and what to look at

Most grasses in a mowed field look alike from a few steps away. The UVM Extension table sorts them by four things: growth habit (a bunch, or a sod that spreads), the seedhead (a spike, a loose panicle, a ball-like clump), the leaf (ligule, auricles, how the new leaf is held in the bud shoot) and a few other marks (a corm, a crimp, grooves). Seedheads are only there for a few weeks in early summer; the leaf marks are there from spring through fall, which is why the leaf features come first below.

This is general reference, not a plant list for any particular land. A forester or botanist should name anything that matters to a plan.

Order of heading

From the Cornell summary: the general order of heading from earliest to latest has been orchardgrass, perennial ryegrass, then reed canarygrass, smooth brome and tall fescue about together, and timothy last (New York trials, 1997). UVM adds that meadow foxtail heads out early. Sweet vernal grass, redtop, red fescue, meadow fescue and quackgrass are not placed in this order by the sources read.

Also in the field

A mowed field has many plants other than grasses. For the wild chervil that the UVM chart lists as a pasture weed, see Wild Chervil; for flowers that feed pollinators in a field, see Pollinator Habitat on Forest and Field. The UVM 2004 pasture chart also names grasses not covered here: barnyardgrass, foxtail and fall panicum (all annuals).
