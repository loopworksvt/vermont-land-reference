Crop tree release (how to do it well)

practicesilviculture

Crop tree release cuts or kills the trees whose crowns touch a chosen tree so it has more light and room. The 2026 Current Use Standards define it as a thinning technique that removes competing trees around selected crop trees to promote their growth and quality (Silvicultural Systems and Treatments places it among the treatments between regenerations). It is a way to put growth on the best trees without a commercial harvest, and the Standards count it, with thinning and timber stand improvement, as an intermediate treatment a plan can list.

Topic
silviculture
Who is involved
landowner, county forester, consulting forester, logger

When it fits

  • A stand with good trees that are crowded. A young or middle-aged stand in which some healthy, well-formed trees have neighbors whose crowns touch theirs.
  • The goal can be timber, wildlife or looks. The USDA Forest Service guide sorts crop trees into timber crop trees (value, growth rate, quality, health), wildlife crop trees (mast production, cavity formation) and aesthetic crop trees (fall color, spring blossoms, size, shape or form), and says one tree may belong to two or all three.
  • No commercial sale is needed. The work can be done by the owner or a small crew. Northern Woodlands' example is a stand with few valuable trees, where a crop tree is "any healthy tree of any species", including trees that give timber, maple sap, food or habitat or that add diversity.
  • Not for every stand. The Forest Service guide says the black cherry crop tree must be young, a dominant or strong codominant, and on a cool, moist site, or it may decline after release; in the Miller red oak trials most released codominant and intermediate trees still dropped a crown class or died within five years (see Risks).

How it is done

1. Choose the crop trees.

  • Health first. Northern Woodlands makes good health the main test (for beech, no signs of beech bark disease).
  • Crown position: crop trees are usually dominant or codominant (a search summary of a University of Kentucky extension guide by Perkey, not read; the Forest Service red oak trials released trees in all classes, and the shaded ones did least well).
  • Species and form. The Forest Service guide's timber notes include quality: black cherry that forks has seams that split in wind and ice and is 'a poor crop tree choice'; trees with grapevines in the crown are more likely to be broken in storms.
  • Spread them out. The only figure for how many is Northern Woodlands' example of 60 crop trees per acre; it recommends no target. (The same magazine's article on pruning, a different job, says 25 to 40 of the best hardwoods per acre; see Pruning Trees.)
  • Mark them so the crew can find them (Marking Trees for Harvest).

2. Decide which trees to cut. Cut only trees whose crowns touch the crop tree's crown; leave all others alone (Northern Woodlands). Do not try to remove every small, marginal tree.

3. Decide how many sides to free.

  • Northern Woodlands: two to four sides depending on species: shade-tolerant sugar maple generally prefers release on two sides; light-loving white pine can be released on all four (Sugar Maple, White Pine).
  • Forest Service guide: a black cherry with a healthy, vigorous crown free to grow on three or four sides can grow 2 to 4 inches in diameter per decade up to 50 years of age; after 50 it may still respond, but less than more tolerant neighbors (Black Cherry).
  • The Miller study of northern red oak found that crown growing space (the share of the crown edge that is free and the distance to competitors) raises diameter growth and crown spread but lowers height growth and the length of clear stem, so freeing more sides is not always better for a clear-log goal (Northern Red Oak).
  • Beech, in the Vermont Landowner's Guide (chapter 9): thin on three sides, west, north and east, and keep trees of any species on the south side to shade the beech and prevent sunscald. Remove beech with sunken lesions or more than half the crown yellow or dead, and any other tree 6 inches or more across that frees a crop tree on those sides; do not girdle beech, because it sprouts and makes a thicket of disease-prone saplings (American Beech).

4. Cut or kill the competitors.

  • Felling is the usual way. Leave the cut material unlopped and unpiled, and leave some cut trees on the ground (Northern Woodlands); lopping and piling tops is time not worth spending.
  • Girdling a few trees per acre makes standing dead trees (snags) for wildlife (Northern Woodlands); the Landowner's Guide says species other than beech can be girdled rather than felled to avoid damaging crop trees or regeneration.
  • Herbicide, if used: the Forest Service guide warns that many hardwoods share root grafts with trees of the same species, so an injected herbicide can pass to the crop tree ('backflash'); choose the product with care, read the label and consult someone experienced. The guide's note on cutting low stumps (a foot or less) is written for getting good sprouts when regenerating a stand, not for release.
  • Tools: the sources read name a chain saw and nothing else.

5. Keep deer and livestock in mind. The Forest Service guide assumes livestock are kept out of the woods and says deer exclosures are often needed where deer are many; a heavy deer herd shifts the advantage to species deer do not eat.

Timing

  • Size: most of the Forest Service guide applies to crop trees at least 25 feet tall. In red oak, response to release was strongest in younger trees and weakened with age, though significant diameter gains were measured in 80-year-old trees (Miller). Black cherry should be released young.
  • Season: the sources read give no season for release in general. For black cherry, do not do work between January and June that leaves cherry slash or standing dead trees, because bark beetles then breed in them and cause gum spots; cherry cut or killed between July and January is much less likely to be used. For beech, work in winter conditions (frozen ground or more than 12 inches of snow) to protect the roots and trunks (Landowner's Guide). Harvest Timing and Seasons covers the other seasonal limits.
  • How long it lasts: the sources read do not say how many years a release lasts; Miller followed red oaks for 5 and 10 years. The Standards' ten-year work schedule is the usual horizon in a plan.

Who does what

The county forester or a consulting forester picks the crop trees, writes the prescription and marks the trees to cut or leave; the owner or a crew does the cutting. For enrolled land the County Forester approves the plan and monitors compliance (Standards glossary) (Selling Timber and Working With Professionals).

Costs and benefits

  • Costs: time and fuel; with no sale there is no income to offset them (Harvest Costs Income and Incentives lists crop tree release among precommercial work that costs money). The sources read give no Vermont price per acre. Northern Woodlands notes that leaving slash and marginal trees saves time and energy.
  • Benefits: released crop trees get room to expand their crowns, which gives them energy to grow and stay healthy; Northern Woodlands says releasing clean beech may encourage mast and a more resistant beech population. The Landowner's Guide (chapter 28) says released oaks can make up to seven times more acorns than unreleased ones and released red oaks twice as many even in a poor acorn year (Wild Turkey). A search summary of Forest Service paper NE-635 reports about 25 percent more diameter growth in trees released on three or four sides than on one or two (not read).

Risks and what to avoid

  • Releasing trees that are too suppressed. In the Miller red oak trials, most released codominant and intermediate stems still dropped a crown class or died within five years, and stump sprouts of red maple and others competed with the released oak.
  • Cutting trees that are not touching the crop tree, and taking too much at once: the Standards limit any single intermediate entry to one-third of the basal area (see Rules).
  • Felling onto crop trees; girdling beech; herbicide that backflashes through root grafts.
  • Releasing a tree that will soon decline: black cherry that is old, forked or in the shade.

Rules that apply

The 2026 Current Use Standards, for intermediate treatments and tending (thinning, crop tree release, timber stand improvement, row thinning): total basal area shall not be reduced below the B-level, and no single entry shall reduce stocking by more than one-third of the basal area; the residual stand should be healthy, vigorous trees with sound structure; Acceptable Growing Stock will not be reduced below the 'C' level in the stocking guide, or, if none is available, 60 square feet of basal area per acre. When a prescription mixes treatment types (the Standards' example is crop tree release and group selection), all prescription elements are required for each treatment type, and plan preparers are encouraged to choose the single type that suits the stand best: ask the county forester (Legal Checks Before a Harvest). Cutting near streams, wetlands or vernal pools has its own rules (Water Quality and Logging Roads).
