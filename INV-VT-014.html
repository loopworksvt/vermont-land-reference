Names

Reed canarygrass (reed canary grass), Phalaris arundinacea, a cool-season perennial grass in the family Poaceae.

Range

Mixed origin. VTinvasives says both Eurasian and native populations exist in the United States, that aggressive strains were planted from the 1800s for forage and erosion control, and that invasive populations may descend from non-native cultivars. Go Botany says New England plants may be a mix of native and introduced types. The fire-effects page cites a genetic study of Vermont and New Hampshire populations pointing to multiple introductions of European cultivars, and says plants cannot reliably be told apart by form, so management should rest on impacts rather than native status. It is present in Vermont, in wetland margins, meadows, fields, riverbanks, shores and disturbed ground.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Size: 40 to 230 cm (Go Botany); VTinvasives says up to about 6 feet.
  • Leaf: flat, blue-green, roughly textured blades up to about 0.75 inch (5 to 20 mm) wide, hairless, tapering gradually. UVM: leaves are wide but the blades are short relative to their width. Rolled in the bud shoot (UVM).
  • Ligule: long and membranous, transparent (VTinvasives); 4 to 11 mm; no auricles (Go Botany, UVM).
  • Seedhead: crowded when young, then spreading as it matures (UVM), 70 to 250 mm long (Go Botany), green, purple or brown, turning tan.
  • Spread: thick, extensive rhizomes, reddish and near the soil surface, and a seed bank (VTinvasives, UVM).
  • Look-alike: native bluejoint (Bluejoint), which has a transparent-white ligule too but is finer in texture, has smaller seeds with hairs, may show dark joints and lacks reddish rhizomes (VTinvasives).

Habitat and spread

VTinvasives lists wetland margins, meadows, fields, riverbanks, shoreland and disturbed areas. Go Botany marks it as usually found in wetlands (FACW). UVM says it grows best in wet soils but tolerates well-drained sites. The fire-effects page gives its New England elevation range as 1 to 1,900 feet, with one observation at 3,200 feet.

Damage

The fire-effects page says invasive populations can form dense stands of a single species and may displace other wetland plants. VTinvasives says it forms dense stands that block sun-loving plants. Neither page says how often this happens in an upland hayfield.

What helps it spread (not something to encourage)

This is an invasive species, so nothing here is advice to encourage it. What the sources read say about where it grows and how it spreads is in the sections above; what they say about stopping it is under Control. The Vermont Landowner's Guide, chapter 17 (corrective strategies for invasive species), covers general methods for invasive plants and pests.

Control (by method, timing, follow-up, what not to do)

The VTinvasives page gives these methods; this library has not checked them against any other source.

  • Small patches: dig up or hand-pull; or cover with black plastic for at least one growing season, watching for rhizomes spreading past the edge.
  • Mowing: twice a year, early to mid-June and early October. Disrupting the roots every two to three weeks weakens plants and depletes the seed bank.
  • Herbicide: the page describes glyphosate applied to cut stems in small populations, in early spring when most native plants are dormant, using a wetland-labelled formulation and following the label; it says certification may be required from the Agency of Agriculture, Food and Markets. Ask the county forester or the Agency before using any herbicide near water.
  • The fire-effects page says reed canarygrass regrows after cutting or mowing, probably from rhizomes and possibly the root crown; it gives no eastern-specific mowing response.

Legal status

Watch list species on the VTinvasives page; not a regulated plant on the Agency of Agriculture quarantine list as read on 2026-10-30. The same pages note that other New England states regulate it differently (Go Botany: invasive in Connecticut and Massachusetts, prohibited in Massachusetts and New Hampshire).
