Waterfowl and migration in Vermont (ducks, geese and mergansers)

guideVermont waterfowl (ducks, geese and mergansers), which species are here in which season, when they migrate, where to see them and what helps them on private land

How to use this guide, and what it does not cover

This guide puts the waterfowl pages in this library in order: who is here in which season (a table from the Vermont Fish & Wildlife checklist), when they come and go, where to see them, what helps them on private land, and what the rules say. Eleven species have their own page with a photograph; the other nine species on the checklist are listed in the table only.

It covers ducks, geese and mergansers. It does not cover grebes, herons, shorebirds or rails, and the Common Loon (a diving water bird, not waterfowl) has its own page, and the sources read give no counts or dates for Vermont's waterfowl in a particular year. For that, ask Vermont Fish & Wildlife or the Vermont Center for Ecostudies. Nothing here is a hunting regulation; the seasons and bag limits change every year.

Only the mallard, American black duck and Canada goose are listed in winter, which fits their habit of staying where there is open water. In early winter, common mergansers gather on large lakes before moving south, though small numbers of adults stay as far north as open water allows (Atlas).

When they come and go (migration patterns)

  • Spring, first back. The Atlas says hooded mergansers generally return in early March, two weeks or more before wood ducks, and may be laying eggs by the end of the month; common mergansers are among the earliest returning waterfowl and look for nest cavities in March; wood ducks return in late March or early April. Mallards and black ducks are early breeders too: a mallard nest with eggs was found on 1 May and black ducks with young as early as 20 May. In spring, many species of waterfowl and songbirds stop at Dead Creek on their way north (Fish & Wildlife brochure).
  • Summer, nesting and broods. Wood duck young have been recorded from 6 May to 5 August, with the peak of hatching in late May and early June; common merganser young from 23 May to 13 July; hooded merganser nests were found from 18 May to 10 June; Canada geese begin nesting in April and the earliest hatching date is 8 May; ring-necked duck broods were seen from 16 June to 23 July (Atlas).
  • Fall, the long migration. The Dead Creek brochure says waterfowl migrations there begin in late September and continue through November; large numbers of snow geese and Canada geese visit in autumn, and snow goose numbers typically peak from late October to early November. The area once attracted about 20,000 snow geese each autumn; shifts in migration routes have reduced the number to about 5,000. Audubon (for the species generally, not for Vermont) says black ducks migrate late, when waters freeze or food runs low, and much of their migration is at night; green-winged teal migrate mostly October to early December; goldeneyes, buffleheads, hooded mergansers and ring-necked ducks migrate relatively late in fall and early in spring.
  • Winter. Open water holds mallards, black ducks and Canada geese. Milder winters have allowed some Vermont Canada geese to stay all year and to begin nesting much earlier than the geese that fly to the tundra to nest (Atlas).
  • Why some birds come and go differently. Pairs of wood ducks, mallards and others form on the wintering grounds, and the males follow the females to the nesting area, so a male's migration can vary from year to year (Audubon). The Atlas says Vermont lies at the northeastern edge of the wood duck's range, at the southeastern edge of the common merganser's, at the southern edge of the ring-necked duck's, and in the core of the hooded merganser's.
  • Flyway. The Atlas accounts discuss Vermont's geese and wood ducks in terms of the Atlantic Flyway (for example, local Canada geese now outnumber migratory ones in the Atlantic Flyway, and the permitted wood duck take is set for the Flyway). The sources read do not describe the flyway's boundaries or routes through Vermont.

Where to see them

  • Dead Creek Wildlife Management Area, Addison (Vermont Fish & Wildlife; the Visitor Center is at 966 Route 17 West, open seasonally and by appointment, 802-759-2397 per the checklist). The area was established in 1950 for breeding and migratory waterfowl; part of it is a refuge that visitors are asked to keep out of at all times, but you may watch from the viewing area pavilion on Route 17. The best time to see geese is in the morning and evening; the best time to see birds in general is early morning (checklist). The brochure calls it one of the best places for birding in Vermont, with duck and goose nesting structures along the creek.
  • Dead Creek is a managed place. Habitats are kept up by periodic mowing and burning of fields, management of water levels with dams and control structures, and farming of some fields. When managers lower the water, shorebirds flock to the exposed mud ("The Addison Phenomenon").
  • Respect the nesting season. The brochure says most birds are nesting and raising young from May to July; keep your distance from nests and nesting structures and use binoculars, and to minimize disturbance of nesting birds from April through June avoid approaching shorelines along narrow wetland channels when boating.

What helps waterfowl on private land (what the sources say)

  • Keep mature trees with cavities near water. Wood ducks, hooded and common mergansers and goldeneyes nest in cavities. The Atlas says Vermont's maturing forests and spreading beavers explain much of the rise in cavity nesters, and that the wood duck's future looks secure if forests keep maturing and are managed on longer rotations to grow sawlogs (Wood Duck, Hooded Merganser, Common Merganser).
  • Let beavers and flooded timber be. Beaver ponds with flooded timber are ideal for hooded mergansers, and wood ducks use woodlands beside beaver ponds (Atlas).
  • Protect wetlands and water quality. The Atlas cites protection of wetlands from pollutants and siltation for wood ducks; a recommendation to maintain high water quality (low turbidity and intact food chains) for hooded mergansers; and stable water levels with emergent, submersed or floating plants and dense herbaceous vegetation for ring-necked ducks. Acid rain reduces prey in unbuffered waters (Ring-necked Duck).
  • Nest boxes: mixed advice. Audubon credits nest boxes with the recovery of wood ducks and says goldeneye numbers have risen where boxes are provided. The Atlas says boxes may produce only 4 to 5 percent of the eastern wood duck population but may increase local densities in marshes that lack nest trees, and sees little need for intensive box programs for hooded mergansers. A box must be placed correctly and checked (the sources read give no box specifications).
  • Do not try to attract Canada geese. The Atlas treats the growing resident population as a problem, with special hunting seasons and egg-and-nest permits (Canada Goose).
  • Harvest near wetlands. See Wildlife Habitat in Harvest Planning and Water Quality and Logging Roads for what the sources say about leaving buffers and cavity trees.

The rules in brief

  • Federal. Native waterfowl are covered by the Migratory Bird Treaty Act (taking, killing, selling and similar acts are prohibited without authorization). The sources read say nothing about nests or cutting trees in nesting season: ask the county forester or the Fish and Wildlife Service before work near an active nest.
  • Hunting seasons. Vermont Fish & Wildlife sets seasons each year for ducks, mergansers, coots, Canada geese, snow geese and brant in three zones: Lake Champlain, Interior Vermont and the Connecticut River. In the 2024-25 season the daily limit was 6 ducks (no more than 4 mallards of which 2 may be hens, 2 black ducks, 3 wood ducks) and 5 mergansers, with separate limits for geese, and a Youth Waterfowl Hunting Weekend on September 28 and 29. Check the current Vermont hunting regulations; these are not rules for this year.
