Names

Black-capped Chickadee, Poecile atricapillus.

Range and habitat

The Atlas found it in nearly every priority block statewide, spread over a broad range of deciduous and coniferous forest. The Boreal Chickadee is the bird of spruce bogs in northeastern Vermont. Audubon lists mixed and deciduous woods, willow thickets and shade trees, open woods and forest edges, and suburbs where nest sites exist.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Size: 5 to 6 inches long (12 to 15 cm), wingspan 6 to 8 inches (Audubon). The sexes look alike.
  • Marks: a black cap and bib, a gray back, buff sides and white cheeks.
  • Voice: a buzzy "chick-a-dee-dee-dee", or a clear whistled "fee-bee" with a lower second note that is often doubled.
  • Look-alikes: the Boreal Chickadee in northeastern spruce bogs; the Carolina Chickadee is nearly identical, so range is the main clue (Audubon).

Role and management

The Atlas says birch and aspen are the most common natural nest substrate, and that nest boxes are accepted more readily when filled with wood shavings, which the pair removes until the cavity is big enough. Eggs are incubated for up to two weeks; the young leave the nest after 16 days and stay with their parents a month or more. Audubon lists insects, seeds and berries, with seeds a larger share in winter. The Atlas says bird-feeder use has probably helped the population and gives no management recommendation.

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

  • Nest cavities in birch and aspen, and winter food. The Atlas says birch and aspen are the most common natural nest substrate, and that nest boxes are accepted more readily when filled with wood shavings, which the pair removes until the cavity is big enough (Paper Birch, Quaking Aspen). Seeds make up more of the diet in winter, and the Atlas says feeders have probably helped the population.
  • Cavity trees (the Guide, den-tree section). Manage for at least six cavity, snag and decadent living trees per acre on average, with one over 18 inches across at breast height and three over 16 inches; leave a selection of different diameters; try to leave two or more large den trees per acre within 300 feet of lakes and ponds; if a den tree or snag must be felled for safety, leave it lying as downed wood. Legal status

Native birds are covered by the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which prohibits taking (killing, capturing, selling, trading and transport are named) protected migratory birds without prior authorization (U.S. The Act applies to native species named in the treaties (50 CFR 10.13). Ask the county forester or the Fish and Wildlife Service before work near an active nest.
