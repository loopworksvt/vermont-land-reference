Pruning trees (how, when, with what, and which trees to prune)

practicepruning

Pruning removes living or dead branches. Two different jobs are described in the sources, and they follow different rules: pruning a yard, shade or fruit tree for safety, health and shape, and pruning crop trees in a woodlot to grow clear, knot-free logs. Both agree on the cut and the tools; they differ on how much to take and why.

Topic
pruning
Who is involved
landowner, consulting forester, arborist

When it fits

  • Yard and shade trees: to remove branches that could fall, that rub or cross, that are dead or diseased, or that grow into a line of sight or a roof. The Forest Service bulletin's order of priority: prune first for safety, next for health, and finally for appearance.
  • Woodlot crop trees (see also Crop Tree Release): only the best trees, to grow clear lumber. Northern Woodlands says to prune only crop trees: the healthiest, most vigorous, straightest trees of species that make good sawtimber, 25 to 40 hardwoods per acre (about 33 to 42 feet apart) or 35 to 60 softwoods per acre (about 27 to 35 feet apart).
  • Not worth it: pruning without first freeing the trees from competition ("pruning is pointless" without thinning, Northern Woodlands); trees over about 9 inches across (the article calls the return "economically dicey"); trees that grow densely and prune themselves.

How it is done

Find the right place to cut. At the base of every branch, look for the branch collar, a shoulder or bulge of stem tissue on the underside, and on the upper side the branch bark ridge, a line of bark that runs along the branch angle. A proper cut starts just outside the branch bark ridge and angles down and away from the trunk, without injuring either. Cut as close to the stem as you can without cutting into the collar (Forest Service). A stub left too long dies and delays wound closure; a flush cut inside the collar injures the trunk and can lead to decay.

Small branches (hand pruners, up to about an inch): use sharp by-pass pruners so the branch is cut cleanly, not crushed.

Branches too heavy to hold: the three-cut method (Forest Service):

  1. A shallow notch on the underside of the branch, a little way out from the collar. It stops the falling branch from tearing a strip of bark down the trunk.
  2. A cut from the top, outside the first, all the way through, leaving a short stub.
  3. Cut the stub off just outside the branch bark ridge and collar.

Dead branches are cut the same way, just outside the ring of new wood that has formed at the base.

Do not top or tip a tree. The bulletin says topping (cutting big upright branches between nodes) and tipping (cutting side branches between nodes) invariably make weakly attached sprouts or kill the branch back to the next lateral. To reduce a crown, cut back to a side branch at least one-third the diameter of the stem removed, and only when absolutely necessary.

Wound dressing: the bulletin says wound dressings and paint do not stop decay, can interfere with the tree's own defenses and are unnecessary in most cases; the exception it gives is a fresh wound on an oak (spring) or an elm (growing season), where dressing is recommended to keep out disease-carrying beetles. Northern Woodlands says dressing is rarely or never helpful.

How much to take.

  • Yard trees (Forest Service): not more than one-quarter of the living crown at a time (spread any more over several years); keep live branches on at least two-thirds of the tree's height; branch diameters: under about 2 inches, go ahead; 2 to 4 inches, think twice; over 4 inches, have a good reason; remove branches with weak V-shaped attachment or included bark; remove rubbing and crossing branches; keep side branches under one-half to three-quarters the diameter of the stem.
  • Woodlot crop trees (Northern Woodlands): remove all dead branches you can reach safely, but no more than one-third of the live branches, and leave one-third to one-half of the tree's height in live branches.

Crop trees, how high. Pruning above 9 feet yields a knot-free log. Twelve feet is a common New England log length; the article's example is pruning as high as 17 feet to make a 16-foot log cut from a one-foot stump. Do it in stages, first with a short-handled saw, then a pole saw. It takes about 10 to 15 minutes a tree.

Which woodlot trees (Northern Woodlands). White pine is the best investment; other softwoods respond less. White ash reliably prunes itself. In hardwoods that grow densely, small branches on red oak, paper birch, yellow birch, black cherry and sugar maple are worth pruning. Leave the outside trees along a field or road unpruned, because their lower branches shelter the stand (White Pine, White Ash, Northern Red Oak, Paper Birch, Yellow Birch, Black Cherry, Sugar Maple).

Tools

Northern Woodlands: for crop trees use a hand pruning saw 18 to 26 inches long with four to eight teeth per inch; never an axe; chain saws are "almost as bad".

Keep tools clean. The bulletin says contaminated tools can carry disease from one tree to the next; pruning in the dormant season greatly reduces the need to disinfect. When needed: dip the tool for 1 to 2 minutes in 70 percent denatured alcohol or one part household bleach to nine of water before each cut, wipe off wood bits, and wash bleach off the metal afterward because it corrodes.

Timing

  • Hardwoods without showy flowers: in the dormant season, usually late fall and winter (the bulletin), so the tree's structure is easy to see, the wound has the whole next growing season to close, disease spread is less likely and sap flow is lower. Northern Woodlands also says late in the dormant season is best for woodlot trees, though pruning is possible any time of year.
  • Conifers: any time of year; pruning in the dormant season may reduce sap and resin flow (Forest Service).
  • Spring-flowering trees and shrubs (the bulletin names redbud and dogwood): right after they flower; summer- or fall-flowering ones: in the dormant season.
  • Fireblight-prone trees (crabapple, hawthorn, pear, mountain ash and others): only in the dormant season.
  • Oaks and elms: the bulletin warns that fresh wounds attract the beetles that spread oak wilt and Dutch elm disease; where oak wilt occurs, avoid pruning oaks in spring and early summer. Ask the county forester or an extension agent what applies in Vermont; the sources read do not say whether oak wilt is present here.
  • Dead branches: any time of year.
  • Woodlot crop trees, by size: start when pine is about 6 inches across, after the first thinning and when the tree is at least 16 feet tall (so a weevil-damaged top still leaves a full sawlog); other species may start earlier, about 3 inches across, leaving plenty of greenery (Northern Woodlands).
  • Apple trees in Vermont: a University of Vermont Extension helpline answer, as reported in a search summary (not read), says March is a good time because the trees are then less likely to suffer winter injury.

Who does what

The owner can do most small pruning from the ground. Hire a certified arborist for large trees, trees near buildings or power lines, or any work off the ground; a consulting forester can say which woodlot trees are crop trees and whether thinning must come first (Silvicultural Systems and Treatments, Selling Timber and Working With Professionals). The sources read do not name a Vermont arborist directory.

Costs and benefits

Benefits (Northern Woodlands): clear, knot-free lumber can be far more valuable than knotty boards; the article cites a Yale School of Forestry study of white pine pruning with a compound return of up to 13 percent on the pruning cost even with the harvest 30 years later, and payback typically takes 25 to 50 years. Costs: time (about 10 to 15 minutes a tree) and tools; the sources read give no dollar prices. For yard trees the bulletin's benefits are safety, health and strength in storms.

Risks and what to avoid

Topping or tipping; flush cuts and long stubs; tearing bark down the trunk (use the three-cut method); taking too much crown; wound paint; pruning when disease-carrying insects are active (oaks in spring, elms in the growing season); working near power lines; using a chain saw above your head or without training; pruning outside trees along a field or road (they shelter the stand).

Rules that apply

None found in the sources read. No source read says whether pruning is restricted in a stream buffer or other protected area, or on land in the Current Use program; ask the county forester before cutting there (Legal Checks Before a Harvest).
