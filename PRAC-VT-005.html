Harvest timing and seasons

practicetiming

When to harvest, and when to hold off, for the soil, the trees, the wildlife and the plan.

Topic
timing
Who is involved
landowner, consulting forester, logger
Related rules
R-VT-004, T-VT-001, S-VT-006, S-VT-003

When it fits

Every harvest. Timing is decided in the contract and watched by the forester, with room for weather.

How it is done

The usual seasons. Most harvesting in Vermont is done in summer and winter. Spring and fall are often too wet. Avoid harvesting when soils are saturated: it harms water quality, leaves muddy, deeply rutted skid trails, moves and loses soil and nutrients, and damages roots in ways that reduce tree health and wood value.

Soils decide. Harvest highly erosive soils and soils that hold moisture only under frozen winter conditions or in the driest summer weather. Slope and aspect change how fast a site dries. Identify rock outcrops, fragile soils and steep slopes inside the harvest area beforehand.

Plan for stoppages. Agree in advance what justifies a temporary shutdown (prolonged wet weather, mud season), the expected length of the job, harvesting and trucking hours (to avoid conflicts with neighbors), the payment schedule and the close-out conditions.

Regeneration timing. If possible time the harvest to a year when the favored species have plentiful seed. Winter cutting protects regenerating trees under deep snow.

Enrolled land. Prescribed treatments may be carried out three years before or after the year in the plan, except activities fixing non-compliance (S-VT-006 Activity Window). A harvest must follow the plan; report it in the annual forest management activity report due February 1 (S-VT-003 FMAR).

Who does what

The forester sets the window with the owner and writes it into the contract; the logger works within it; the owner accepts a stoppage when conditions require it.

Timing

See above. A reasonable plan allows time ahead for the plan itself, an amendment (if needed), the legal checks and the sale (Legal Checks Before a Harvest).

Costs and benefits

Cutting on frozen ground or in dry weather protects soil, trees and water and avoids repair costs, but limits the available days. Wet-season work can mean ruts, higher equipment wear (the AMP booklet notes muddy roads raise maintenance costs and cut equipment life) and penalties if there is a discharge (Water Quality and Logging Roads).

Risks and what to avoid

Working wet soils; ignoring nesting windows; starting before the plan or amendment is approved on enrolled land (T-VT-001 Cutting Contrary to the Plan).

Rules that apply

The nesting and denning windows above are guidance from Fish & Wildlife, not rules, unless a permit or the Wetland Rules apply; the Wetland Rules and the water-quality practices are legal requirements (Legal Checks Before a Harvest).

Not recorded here

Specific dates for mud season or frost depth at any elevation, a sugaring-season conflict (taps and sap season), and bat roost windows (the sources read say only to leave snags standing).
