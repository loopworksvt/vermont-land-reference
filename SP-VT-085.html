Names

American Woodcock, Scolopax minor.

Range and habitat

The Atlas says the preferred habitat is damp soil in alder swamps and brushy fields, and young, brushy forest; the main concern is that this habitat is maturing across the Northeast, and the causes of change since the first atlas are unknown. The species was found across all of Vermont in the first atlas, but the Atlas says it may have been underestimated. Audubon lists forest thickets and moist woods by day and open fields and pastures at night.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Size: 10 to 12 inches long (25 to 31 cm), wingspan 16.5 to 18.9 inches; about the size of a robin (Audubon).
  • Marks: a round body, short neck and legs, a long bill and eyes set far back on the head; an orange-buff belly, black bars across the crown and a camouflaged brown, gray and tan back.
  • Voice: a loud, buzzy "bzeep!" repeated on the ground about every two seconds in courtship.

Role and management

Audubon lists mostly earthworms and soil insect larvae, plus millipedes, spiders and snails. Young-forest management: the Atlas recommends "retain and expand forest openings" and setting back succession on maturing forestland; it does not specifically mention clearcuts. The Atlas notes that for atlasers who survey only in June or July the chance of meeting a woodcock is remote.

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

  • Two kinds of cover, in about 25 acres. The Guide says woodcock do not need large tracts: swales of alder, dogwood and willow where they feed, nest and raise young, plus recently abandoned fields or forest openings for roosting and courtship flights; the singing grounds are surrounded by shrubs such as blueberry, goldenrod and red-osier dogwood.
  • Food: earthworms and other invertebrates in rich, moist loam and sandy loam along water, floodplains and the edges of beaver flowages; a woodcock can eat up to 90 percent of its body weight in a day, so management should focus on the feeding areas.
  • What to do (the Guide): alder and dogwood lose much of their value after about 20 years, so clearcut 25 percent of the feeding area every five years, in strips 10 to 20 feet wide or quarter-acre patches; keep nearby openings grassy by mowing or brush-hogging every 3 to 5 years (burning and pasturing may also work, with less control); abandoned fields are preferred where they exist.
  • Timing: woodcock arrive as early as March; chicks hatch in late May and early June; they leave between mid-October and early November when the ground freezes.

Legal status

Native birds are covered by the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which prohibits taking (killing, capturing, selling, trading and transport are named) protected migratory birds without prior authorization (U.S. The Act applies to native species named in the treaties (50 CFR 10.13). Ask the county forester or the Fish and Wildlife Service before work near an active nest.
