Names

Black-eyed Susan (black-eyed coneflower), Rudbeckia hirta, in the family Asteraceae.

Range and habitat

Go Botany lists human-disturbed areas, meadows and fields, and woodlands, and records the plant as present in Vermont. It answers "Yes and no (some introduced)" on native status and says that Rudbeckia hirta var. hirta is a rare native, while var. pulcherrima is introduced; for Vermont it lists var. hirta as historical and var. pulcherrima as non-native. Go Botany marks the plant as usually in non-wetlands (FACU). Minnesota Wildflowers calls the species native there and lists sun; fields, prairies, open woods, roadsides and disturbed soil.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Flower head: a domed center of more than 50 tiny disk flowers 10 to 20 mm across, ringed by 6 to 25 rays 15 to 45 mm long. Go Botany lists the ray colors as yellow, orange, pink to red, or green to brown, and the disk colors as yellow, blue to purple, or green to brown; the common field form has yellow rays and a dark center.
  • Leaves: simple and alternate, with toothed edges and a pointed tip; blades 30 to 300 mm long and 10 to 70 mm wide in shapes from narrow to broad; hairy underneath. The lower leaves are larger and toothier than the upper ones.
  • Look-alikes (Go Botany): Rudbeckia fulgida and Rudbeckia bicolor; the page does not say how to tell them apart.

Role and management

The sources read give no mowing, grazing or control advice for this plant, and no statement about its value to wildlife.

What helps it thrive (light, soil and moisture, and how it is kept)

  • Light: full sun to partial shade; it thrives in the open, sunny conditions created by fire (FEIS).
  • Soil and water: clayey loam to sandy loam soils with low to moderate water needs; found in open woods, sunny roadsides and meadows and disturbed places.
  • Spread and disturbance: reproduces by seed and sprouts from the root crown; it is a pioneer that can dominate early in succession. Effects of fire varied (profuse on unburned plots in one study, more seedlings after burning in another); the review gives no mowing response.
  • Use to others: pollinated by bees and flies.
