Names

Orchardgrass, Dactylis glomerata, a grass in the family Poaceae. Also written orchard grass.

Range and habitat

Not native. Go Botany says it is native to Europe and grows in meadows, fields, roadsides and cultivated land; it is present in Vermont. The fire-effects page says it was introduced to the eastern United States from Europe in 1760, is shade tolerant, and has "naturalized to fields, meadows, and waste places". It says it does not usually persist past one or two decades and is susceptible to replacement by native species. It adds that orchardgrass can invade alfalfa stands in the northeastern United States.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Growth habit (UVM): a tall, bunch-forming grass that tillers freely; long leaves that look lighter green than other grasses; leaves strongly folded in the bud shoot.
  • Ligule: long and pointed, with no auricles (UVM); membranous, 3 to 11 mm (Go Botany). The sheath is a closed tube with no hairs (Go Botany).
  • Leaf: blades 2 to 10 mm wide and up to 35 cm long, flat, slightly folded or rolled, hairless and rough (Go Botany). Large, prominent tufts of basal leaves.
  • Seedhead: a branched panicle 40 to 200 mm long with the spikelets in ball-like clumps (UVM) mainly at the branch ends (Go Botany). Spikelets are 5 to 8 mm long with 2 to 6 florets.
  • Height: 50 to 210 cm (Go Botany).
  • Look-alikes: Go Botany lists none. The UVM table separates it from the fescues by its folded bud shoot, long ligule and lack of auricles; tall fescue and meadow fescue have rolled bud shoots, short ligules and short auricles (Tall Fescue, Meadow Fescue).

Role in the field

A hay and pasture grass. In the Cornell trials it was the earliest of the common hay grasses to head (the general order given is orchardgrass, then perennial ryegrass, then reed canarygrass, smooth brome and tall fescue about together, and timothy last).

What helps it thrive (light, soil and moisture, and how it is kept)

  • Light: shade tolerant (FEIS).
  • Soil and water: best adapted to well-drained, rich or moderately fertile soils with enough water; responds well to nitrogen; found in relatively cool, moist places and forest openings.
  • Keeping it: reproduces mostly by seed and tillering, with most seed germinating in the fall; it does not usually persist past one or two decades; do not clip below 3 inches; it does not withstand continuous heavy grazing, and light early-season grazing is the most beneficial.
  • Use to others: highly palatable to deer; the green basal rosette is good winter forage for deer.

Managing for it and living with it

For forage, the fire-effects page says not to clip below 3 inches and that cutting at 2- to 3-week intervals gave as much growth as cutting at 5- to 7-week intervals. That is forage advice, not advice for a field mowed once a year. Nothing read treats it as a problem plant.

Legal status
