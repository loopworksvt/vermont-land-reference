Names

Common yarrow, Achillea millefolium, in the family Asteraceae. In New England Go Botany treats the plants as the subspecies lanulosa.

Range and habitat

Go Botany answers "Yes" to native to North America and says New England collections "largely represent our native subspecies (lanulosa)"; introduced plants of the Old World subspecies "appear to be confined to garden settings". It lists alpine and subalpine zones, human-disturbed habitats, cliffs and ledges, meadows and fields, mountain summits and shores, and records the plant as present in Vermont. It marks the plant as usually in non-wetlands (FACU). Minnesota Wildflowers lists part shade or sun; fields, prairies, open woods and roadsides.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Flower heads: small, in groups of two or more in a flat-topped cluster. Each head has white five-lobed disk flowers (6 to 20) and 1 to 10 rays that are white or pink-red and only 1 to 4 mm long (Go Botany).
  • Leaves: alternate, 30 to 350 mm long, narrow in outline and divided into many small lobes, at least 15 leaflets or primary divisions, which gives the feathery look.
  • Look-alike (Go Botany): Achillea ligustica usually has 10 or fewer lobes in one plane and egg-shaped blades; common yarrow has 15 or more divisions that are not in one plane and narrow blades.

Role and management

The sources read give no mowing, grazing or control advice for this plant, and no statement about its value to wildlife.

What helps it thrive (light, soil and moisture, and how it is kept)

  • Light: intolerant of dense or excessive shade (FEIS).
  • Soil and water: common on thin soils and sandy gravelly loam on open flats and dry meadows; usually on dry, open sites, including roadsides and brushland and aspen openings.
  • Spread and disturbance: spreads from rhizome fragments and short-distance wind-borne seed; it increases rapidly in disturbed areas and overgrazed ground, and late spring burning usually reduces it. The review gives no mowing response.
  • Use to others: an important food of young sage grouse chicks in the West; deer and others graze the flower heads.

Legal status

Not on the Agency of Agriculture quarantine list as read on 2026-10-31 (effective 2026-06-16; the list's Watch List was not read). No other legal status was looked up. The quarantine list covers introduced plants.
