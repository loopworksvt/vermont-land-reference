Names

Bluejoint (Canada bluejoint, Canada reed grass), Calamagrostis canadensis, a grass in the family Poaceae.

Range and habitat

Native. Go Botany lists marshes, meadows and fields, shores of rivers and lakes, swamps and wetland margins; it is present in Vermont. The fire-effects page says it grows on moist to wet sites, most often on peat soils, and tolerates acid conditions. Because Go Botany marks it as wetland-only and also lists "meadows and fields", expect it in the wet part of a field rather than on dry ground.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Growth habit (Go Botany): upright stems 32 to 180 cm tall, singly or a few together, forming diffuse colonies; rhizomes present.
  • Leaf: blades 10 to 50 cm long and 2 to 11 mm wide, flat or slightly folded, hairless but rough; ligule membranous, 1 to 12 mm; no auricles.
  • Seedhead: a loose, spreading panicle 60 to 250 mm long, with one-flowered spikelets 2 to 5.2 mm long and a short awn (0.9 to 3.1 mm).
  • Look-alike: reed canarygrass. VTinvasives says bluejoint has a transparent-white ligule like it, but is finer in texture, has smaller seeds with hairs for wind dispersal, can have dark joints (nodes), and lacks the reddish rhizomes (Reed Canarygrass).

Role in the field

The fire-effects page says it flowers prolifically on recently disturbed sites and that its rhizomes help stabilize streambanks. Fire kills the top growth; plants resprout from rhizomes after low-severity fire.

What helps it thrive (light, soil and moisture, and how it is kept)

  • Light: the review gives no light need; it grows from lowland wet sites to semishaded woodlands (FEIS).
  • Soil and water: prefers moist sites but survives a wide range of moisture; it cannot germinate in drought but is very drought resistant once established; imperfectly to moderately well drained, mostly peat but also mineral soils; tolerates very acidic soils (pH as low as 3.5).
  • Keeping it: an aggressive early colonizer of disturbed ground by wind-borne seed and an extensive rhizome network; yields fell 15 to 20 percent when cut two to four times compared with cutting once; sensitive to overgrazing, so limit grazing when soils are wet, especially near streams; light surface burning tends to increase it. It is a serious competitor of conifer seedlings on disturbed moist sites, and its rhizomes help stabilize streambanks.
  • Use to others: a large amount of forage for big game and livestock; elk use it in winter.

Managing for it and living with it

The fire-effects page does not state a response to mowing. It reports, from a hay study, that yields fell 15 to 20 percent with 2 to 4 cuts a season, 35 to 45 percent with 5 to 6 cuts and about 70 percent with 7 cuts, compared with one cut at the end of the season.

Legal status

Native. Not on the Agency of Agriculture quarantine list as read on 2026-10-30. One variety is extremely rare in Vermont and another historical (Go Botany); the common plant is not.
