Names

Balsam woolly adelgid (BWA), Adelges piceae. A tiny sap-feeding insect that attacks true firs. It was brought to the northeastern United States around 1900, most likely on infested nursery stock.

Range

Reported in all Vermont counties; the page says it has spread throughout the state. FPR's 2023 aerial survey mapped 400 acres of fir dieback and mortality attributed to it, compared with 3,945 acres in 2022 (FPR 2023 report). Hosts: balsam fir in the Northeast (and other true firs elsewhere).

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

The insect is visible only as a white, woolly spot. The page lists a heading for look-alikes but names none. Symptoms are in the calendar. Overwintering stage: immature nymph; two generations a year are possible depending on temperature.

Habitat and spread

It spreads on infested nursery stock and freshly cut fir material.

Damage

Fir mortality, especially on upland sites and among larger-crowned trees; reduced species diversity and age structure, with effects on animals that use fir, such as moose, snowshoe hares and songbirds (Moose, Snowshoe Hare, Balsam Fir). FPR's list of causes lists "fir decline" statewide as related to this adelgid.

What helps it spread (not something to encourage)

This is an invasive species, so nothing here is advice to encourage it. What the sources read say about where it grows and how it spreads is in the sections above; what they say about stopping it is under Control. The Vermont Landowner's Guide, chapter 17 (corrective strategies for invasive species), covers general methods for invasive plants and pests.

Control (by method, timing, follow-up, what not to do)

The page gives these points for landowners:

  • Balsam fir can still be grown in infested areas.
  • If fir is dying, consider BWA as a cause even when signs are faint.
  • A shorter rotation may be justified, since vulnerability rises with stand age.
  • Fir can be regenerated after the overstory trees are killed.
  • Do not ship live fir or freshly cut woody material to regions without BWA.
  • The page points to a Vermont Forest Health leaflet (not read).

No treatment is described on the page read.

Legal status

The page says that in North America only Michigan has a quarantine regulating fir imports; Vermont's legal status is not stated.
