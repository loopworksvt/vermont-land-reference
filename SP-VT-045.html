Names

Poverty oatgrass (poverty grass), Danthonia spicata, a grass in the family Poaceae.

Range and habitat

Native. Go Botany lists meadows and fields, grassland, disturbed ground, ledges, summits, talus and rocky slopes and woodlands, on dry-mesic to dry, often sandy or thin soil; it is present in Vermont. The fire-effects page calls it a native, perennial, cool-season bunchgrass of low-fertility, sandy or rocky, well-drained soils in old fields, pastures, roadsides and woodland margins, a common pioneer on northeastern coastal sandplains and old sheep pastures, and a common secondary successor on burned or cut sites, old fields and old pasture.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Growth habit (Go Botany): a small bunchgrass, 7 to 100 cm tall, with no rhizomes. Large tufts of basal leaves; current and dried old basal leaves are curly.
  • Leaf: blades 6 to 20 cm long and only 0.8 to 4 mm wide, usually rolled inward and curling later in the season; the ligule is a fringe of fine hairs 0.2 to 1 mm long; no auricles.
  • Seedhead: a short panicle only 20 to 50 mm long, with 1 to 3 large spikelets per branch (7 to 15 mm long); the lemma awn is 5 to 8 mm long and coiled at least half a turn.
  • Look-alike: flattop oatgrass (Danthonia compressa) has flat leaves that usually do not curl and a taller, spreading seedhead 5 to 10 cm long; poverty oatgrass has rolled, curling leaves and a shorter, upright seedhead of 2 to 5 cm (Go Botany).

Role in the field

The fire-effects page says active grazing usually increases its abundance because its flowers near the base are not removed by cattle, and that its seeds can stay viable in soil for decades until disturbance triggers new plants. It is called a common erosion controller on burned areas and clearcuts in the eastern United States.

What helps it thrive (light, soil and moisture, and how it is kept)

  • Light: grows best in high light and decreases as competition for light increases (FEIS).
  • Soil and water: low-fertility, sandy or rocky, well-drained soils with low soil moisture, including acidic soils (pH 4.5 to 4.7) and sites with poor nutrient and moisture regimes.
  • Keeping it: it establishes by tillering and from seed after top-kill, and its seeds may stay in the soil for decades; active grazing usually favors it; populations often increase the season after fire but some grasslands lose it after frequent disturbance. Found in old fields, pastures, roadsides, woodland margins, clearcuts and burns.
  • Use to others: limited, early-season use; fair cover for small mammals, small nongame birds and upland game birds.

Managing for it and living with it

The fire-effects page does not state a response to mowing. The sources read give no Vermont management advice.

Legal status

Native. Not on the Agency of Agriculture quarantine list as read on 2026-10-30. Rare-plant status not looked up.
