Names

White-breasted Nuthatch, Sitta carolinensis.

Range and habitat

The Atlas profile says it nests in natural hollows in snags and old woodpecker holes, since it cannot excavate its own, and lists forest maturation as a benefit and adjusting well to human presence (with a question mark) as causes of change. Audubon lists mature deciduous or mixed forest, woodland edges, and suburbs or parks with large trees.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Size: 5 to 6 inches long (13 to 15 cm) (Audubon).
  • Marks: a white face and chest, a gray back and a narrow dark crown stripe (black on males, often gray on females); some show orange-brown on the lower belly. Chunky and short-tailed, with a thin bill and a beady black eye that stands out on the white face.
  • Voice: the call is a nasal "yank-yank"; the song is a series of low whistled notes.

Role and management

Audubon lists mostly insects and spiders in summer and more seeds in winter, and suet and peanut-butter mixes at feeders. Because it depends on cavities in snags and old woodpecker holes (Pileated Woodpecker), standing dead trees are the habitat feature the Atlas points to; it gives no recommendation.

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

  • Standing dead trees and old woodpecker holes. It depends on cavities in snags (Pileated Woodpecker); the Atlas points to standing dead trees as the habitat feature but gives no recommendation. Suet and peanut-butter mixes at feeders (Audubon).
  • Cavity trees (the Guide, den-tree section). Manage for at least six cavity, snag and decadent living trees per acre on average, with one over 18 inches across at breast height and three over 16 inches; leave a selection of different diameters; try to leave two or more large den trees per acre within 300 feet of lakes and ponds; if a den tree or snag must be felled for safety, leave it lying as downed wood. Pileated Woodpecker records the department's targets in full.

Legal status

Native birds are covered by the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which prohibits taking (killing, capturing, selling, trading and transport are named) protected migratory birds without prior authorization (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service page, read through a summarizing fetch 2026-10-31). The Act applies to native species named in the treaties (50 CFR 10.13); that list was not checked for this species, and the page read says nothing about nests or about cutting trees in nesting season. Ask the county forester or the Fish and Wildlife Service before work near an active nest.
