Harvest costs, income and incentives

practicecosts

What a harvest costs, what it returns and who pays whom. The honest answer is that the Vermont sources read do not publish typical forester fees or logging costs; they publish how the money flows and one regional price report. This note gives the structure and the limits.

Topic
costs
Who is involved
landowner, consulting forester, logger, county forester
Related rules
S-VT-003

When it fits

Before deciding on a harvest or a plan, and when comparing a bid, a forester's quote or a cost-share offer.

How it is done

Money in (for the owner):

  • Stumpage: the value of the uncut timber as it stands. Most owners sell stumpage: the buyer harvests and processes. Paid as a lump sum (agreed price for the trees, from an estimate) or mill scale (per tree cut, measured at the mill) per Selling Timber and Working With Professionals.
  • Units: saw logs by the thousand board feet (MBF; a board foot is a 12 by 12 by 1 inch board), pulp and firewood by the cord (a 4 by 4 by 8 foot stack), chips and some pulp by the ton.
  • What decides the price: species, size, health and number of trees; how easy they are to remove; the products they make (saw logs, veneer, pulp, chips, firewood); market conditions. The brochure's advice: bring in a professional, as for selling a house.
  • Competitive bidding (arranged by the forester) is said to improve the price.

Money out (for the owner):

  • A consulting forester's fee for the plan, inventory, marking, sale administration and supervision. FPR says foresters work for a fee that is usually well worth the investment, and to get the cost in writing. Common arrangements (a search summary of the Vermont Woodlands Association): a flat fee for services such as plans, an hourly rate, or a percentage of harvest revenue. No percentage or dollar figure was found in a Vermont source read (rechecked 2026-10-31; the Vermont Woodlands Association page names the three methods and gives no number). The only figures found are from other states, below.
  • Access and infrastructure (new skid trails, landing work): settle in the contract who pays so there are no misunderstandings (Guidelines 2.1).
  • Close-out (waterbars, seeding, mulching): the logger does it under the contract; the owner maintains erosion devices afterward.
  • Permits and filing fees where required (Legal Checks Before a Harvest).
  • Precommercial work (thinning small trees, crop tree release, invasive control) costs money without a sale.

Fee and cost figures from outside Vermont (rechecked 2026-10-31). None is a Vermont rate; they show the range quoted by extension services elsewhere, and a different timber market. Use them only to see whether a Vermont quote is in the same range.

Money to be careful with: the contract's bond from the logger (cash bond, bank draft or letter of credit) protects against unfinished work; the owner should hold it until close-out is done.

Benefits other than money: forest health and regeneration, wildlife habitat (Wildlife Habitat in Harvest Planning), keeping the land in Use Value Appraisal (the plan must be followed), and the long-term value of the remaining trees. Releasing the best trees speeds their growth (Silvicultural Systems and Treatments).

Incentives: FPR's cost-share page says program offerings change over time and points to a landowner-resources page; it names none. The Landowner's Guide mentions that NRCS may help with habitat work through Farm Bill programs. The forester can coordinate the paperwork. Rates, eligibility and deadlines are not recorded here; ask the county forester or NRCS.

Tax: timber income is generally treated as capital gain when the sale transfers an interest in the timber, depending on the contract structure (search summary); a land gains tax does not apply to a sale of timber unless the property is 300 or more acres (same summary); the Guidelines point to www.timbertax.org. Basis and depletion are calculated with the forester's help. Ask a tax professional.

Who does what

The forester estimates value, runs the bidding and checks the volume paid for; the logger pays the owner on the schedule; the owner holds the bond and pays the forester.

Timing

Payments follow the contract's schedule, often tied to the percentage of acres or volume harvested; the bond is returned after close-out. Cost-share applications have their own deadlines (not recorded).

Costs and benefits

What the program itself requires (2026 Standards, read as text). Unless a parcel is in Reserve Forestland, a minimum of 20 acres must be "actively managed for forest products" (section 2.1). The ten-year work schedule lists "all prescribed commercial harvesting cuttings, non-commercial treatments and any other treatments necessary to comply", each with a single year, and dates may move three years either way. The plan forms allow "none prescribed" for an earlier plan's activity. The Standards text read does not say whether a plan with only non-commercial treatments (crop tree release, thinning small trees) meets "actively managed for forest products"; that is a question for the county forester, and the answer decides how much of the cost and disturbance of a commercial harvest comes with enrollment.

As above. A simple worksheet an owner can fill in with quotes: expected gross stumpage from the forester's estimate minus the forester's fee, any owner-paid infrastructure, permits, and set-aside for erosion-control maintenance; then add tax effects and any cost-share.

Risks and what to avoid

Judging a price from one report; paying a logger's invoice for work not done; a contract with no bond; treating cost-share rates found online as current.

Rules that apply

Contract and tax law; Current Use rules if enrolled (a harvest must follow the plan; report it by February 1, S-VT-003 FMAR).

Not recorded here

Typical Vermont forester and logger charges (only other states' commission ranges are above); current stumpage (the latest FPR report is the 2023 first-to-third-quarter one); cost-share payment rates; carbon or other payment programs; the effect of Current Use on income tax.
