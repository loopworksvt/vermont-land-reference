Names

Spring Peeper, Pseudacris crucifer.

Range and habitat

Adults live in herbaceous vegetation or woods next to heavily vegetated swamps and marshes of all sizes (both pages). Mating is in water. The Atlas gives no distribution description beyond a records map.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Size: one of Vermont's smallest frogs, about 1 to 1.5 inches (Vermont Fish & Wildlife); the Atlas gives 1.3 inches for a large adult.
  • Color and marks: light to dark brown, with dark markings on the back that usually form an "X"; no dorsolateral ridges; small adhesive discs on the toes. Breeding males have black throats and swollen thumbs, and males are smaller than females.
  • Voice: a short ascending whistle or a series of ascending peeps.

Role and management

Both pages say nocturnal spring peepers eat small invertebrates and hibernate under logs or behind loose tree bark until spring thaw. A female lays hundreds of eggs, singly or in packages of two or three attached to vegetation, and the tadpoles metamorphose within two to three months. The state page says the population has been mapped statewide and benefits from the department's habitat conservation work; neither page gives landowner advice, and the Atlas asks for reports of sightings.

Legal status

Not stated on the pages read (the "Least Concern" tag is a conservation status, not a legal one).
