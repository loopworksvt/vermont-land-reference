Harvest methods and equipment

practiceharvest

How logs get from the stump to the truck, and the machines involved. The harvest method is the form the wood is in when it reaches the landing; the harvest system is the combination of machines. Many combinations can do the same harvest; the choice fits the product, the terrain and the soil.

Topic
harvest
Who is involved
consulting forester, logger, landowner

When it fits

Chosen with the forester and logger from the product being cut (saw timber, pulp, firewood, chips), the terrain (manual cutting is still used on steep, rugged ground where machines are impractical) and the soils (see Harvest Timing and Seasons). The guidelines say to use the equipment that best suits the site and keeps disturbance and damage to the remaining stand low.

How it is done

Three methods

  • Whole-tree: trees are cut with stem, branches and top intact and moved to the landing, where the tops are turned into chips or returned to the harvest area.
  • Cut-to-length: trees are cut into final product lengths at or near the stump (logs, pulp, firewood, chip wood) and delivered pre-processed.
  • Tree-length: trees are delimbed and topped at the stump and delivered as full-length stems, processed to length at the landing or the mill.

Common machines (sizes as printed in the guidelines)

MachineWhat it does
ChainsawManual felling; still used on steep terrain
Feller buncherTracked machine with a cutting head that fells and gathers several stems and carries them to the trail; 8 to 11 feet wide, cuts stems up to 24 inches, reaches 20 to 25 feet from the trail center
Harvester or processorFells (or only processes), delimbs, tops and cuts to product length; 8 to 10 feet wide, stems up to 20 inches, reach up to 33 feet
Skidder (cable or grapple)Drags trees to the landing; cable skidders pair with chainsaw felling (tree-length); grapple skidders pair with feller bunchers (whole-tree) and can haul tops back to spread in the woods
ForwarderCarries logs on its bunk to the landing, usually with a harvester (cut-to-length); 8 to 9 feet wide; tracks over the tires give better traction and lower soil impact
Delimber, loader and slasher, chipper or grinderLanding machines that remove branches, cut to length, and process tops and limbs into biomass chips

Common systems: whole-tree (feller buncher, grapple skidder, delimber, loader and slasher); cut-to-length (harvester or processor with forwarder; or chainsaw with forwarder); tree-length (chainsaw with cable skidder). A small bulldozer can bunch logs to a main skid trail to keep larger skidders on well-built, well-drained trails.

Protecting what stays: damage to remaining trees (broken tops, scuffed bark, exposed or severed roots) and seedlings is minimized by skilled operators, mechanical harvesters that place the trees, bumper trees left along skid trails (especially on corners) and sensible trail layout.

Who does what

The forester designs and monitors the layout; the logger chooses and runs the system; the landowner agrees the layout and who pays for new infrastructure such as skid trails so there are no later surprises (Selling Timber and Working With Professionals).

Timing

Soil conditions limit machines: harvest highly erosive soils and moisture-retaining soils only under frozen winter conditions or the driest summer conditions. Slope and aspect change how fast soils dry. See Harvest Timing and Seasons.

Costs and benefits

The sources give no costs. Qualitatively: mechanized systems have higher output (their use has grown over 30 years) and put the operator in a cab; manual felling remains needed on steep ground; forwarders on tracks have lower soil impact; whole-tree harvesting yields chips for biomass but removes more of the tops and limbs, which add nutrients and protect trails when returned to the woods.

Risks and what to avoid

Soil compaction and rutting, damage to residual trees and regeneration, equipment delivered with soil or invasive plant material, and fuel or fluid leaks. The guidelines say the forester inspects equipment on arrival to ensure it is relatively clean, free of soil and invasive plant material and in good working condition, with a fire extinguisher (Harvest Close-out and Follow-up, Water Quality and Logging Roads).

Rules that apply

Water-quality practices apply to any system (Water Quality and Logging Roads); worker-safety standards (VOSHA and OSHA logging standards) apply to the contractor (Legal Checks Before a Harvest).

Not recorded here

Cost or production per machine, carbon or fuel use, cable or skyline systems for steep ground, and horse logging.
