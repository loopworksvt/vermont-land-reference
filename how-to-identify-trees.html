How to identify trees (by growth stage)

guidetree identification by growth stage

A reference for telling the common Vermont trees apart at different stages of growth: as seedlings, as saplings and poles, as large trees, and in winter without leaves. It is general reference. A forest management plan needs trees and regeneration named by a forester; this guide helps an owner see what is likely there and ask better questions. The species notes (links below) carry the detail for each tree.

The stages and their sizes

Size classes are used in Vermont plans, so two sources are given.

DBH is diameter at breast height, 4.5 feet above the ground. For a plan, use the UVA Standards' classes. The two sources differ for seedlings and saplings, so ask which one a table uses. The seedling guide's "class one" and "class two" mean the smallest, youngest seedlings and the larger, more developed ones.

A first sort: what you see decides

1. Needles or scales (conifer) or flat leaves (broadleaf)?

  • Conifers
    • Needles in bundles of five, 3 to 5 inches, blue-green: White Pine.
    • Flat single needles about 1/2 inch with two white lines underneath, mostly in two ranks, small cones that hang: Eastern Hemlock.
    • Flat needles about 3/4 inch, shiny dark green above and silvery-blue below, upright cones, bark with resin blisters: Balsam Fir.
    • Four-sided, sharply pointed needles each on a small woody peg, 1/2 to 5/8 inch: Red Spruce.
    • Scale-like leaves in flattened sprays, fibrous reddish bark: Northern White-cedar.
  • Broadleaf trees: go to step 2.

2. Are the leaves (and so the twigs and buds) paired opposite each other, or alternate?

  • Opposite: the maples (Sugar Maple, Red Maple, Striped Maple) and the ash (White Ash).
    • Simple lobed leaves: a maple. Leaves divided into leaflets: an ash.
    • On a very small seedling the stem can be so short that alternate leaves look opposite. Leaves of clearly different sizes suggest the arrangement is really alternate (the seedling guide says this is not always reliable).
  • Alternate: everything else here. Next look at the leaf edge and veins:
    • Each straight vein ends in a sharp tooth, bark smooth and gray: American Beech.
    • Deep lobes ending in bristle tips: Northern Red Oak.
    • Doubly toothed ("toothed twice"), pointed oval leaves: the birch family (Yellow Birch, Paper Birch) and American Hophornbeam.
    • Finely toothed, glossy, oblong leaves with a bitter-almond smell from a scratched twig: Black Cherry.
    • Nearly round, heart-shaped leaves on a flattened stalk: Quaking Aspen.

Quick separations that trip people up

PairThe separating clues (from the sources read)
Sugar maple and red maple (leaves)Sugar: 5 rounded lobes, smooth edge, paler underneath. Red: 3 to 5 lobes with a toothed (serrated) edge, whitish underneath. A UNH Extension summary adds U-shaped gaps between lobes on sugar and V-shaped on red, and tan buds on sugar versus bright red on red (search summary, not read in full).
Red maple and mountain maple, youngHard to tell apart when very young. Mountain maple often has sunken veins. When in doubt, record "maple" and photograph it.
Striped maple, any ageThree-lobed "goose-foot" leaf; small, frequent teeth and a tapering tip; young bark gray-green with white lengthwise stripes; twigs green or red.
Beech seedlings among sugar mapleBeech has straight, parallel, unbranched veins each ending in a tooth. Beech seedlings hide in sugar maple thickets.
Black cherry and serviceberry or beech, smallCherry: small stout brown buds, fine shallow teeth pointing toward the tip, lenticels on twigs, sometimes orange fuzz at the base of the midrib, almond smell from the scratched bark.
Yellow birch and the other birchesScratch a small patch of twig: wintergreen smell means yellow birch or black birch. Otherwise young birches are very hard to tell apart; look at what grows nearby and record "birch".
White ash and the other ashesHabitat (white ash on well-drained sites; black ash in wet ones) and leaf scar shape. Seedlings are nearly impossible to name to species where two or three ashes grow together.
Red oak and black oakPointed lobe tips mean the red oak group. Red oak buds are conical, red-brown and mostly hairless; black oak buds are angular, fuzzy and buff. They hybridize.
Hemlock, balsam fir, red spruce, white pine seedlingsHemlock: needles under about 1.75 cm, in loose threes at first. Balsam fir: needles 2 cm or longer, two white lines underneath. Red spruce: wispy, sharp needles pointing toward the twig tip. White pine: needles in bundles of five on bigger seedlings. When only cotyledons are out, white pine is taller and sturdier than the tiny red spruce.
A conifer seedling and a clubmossClubmosses (Huperzia) branch in equal forks (Y shapes) and have an underground stem linking plants; conifer seedlings do not.

Seedling tips (from the New England seedling guide)

  • Venation is one of the most reliable characters on a young seedling; leaf shape alone is variable.
  • Look at the twig: the color of old and new growth and the shape of leaf scars help.
  • Photograph from several angles, note which mature trees stand nearby, and if you are not sure, record the genus ("maple", "ash", "birch") rather than a species. The state's plan tables accept the species in order of success, so honest "genus only" entries are better than wrong ones.
  • Class one ash seedlings often have simple leaves; the first compound leaves have a much larger end leaflet.
  • Hickory, birch and ash seedlings are among the hardest to name to species.
  • By forest type: northern hardwood forest (below about 2,700 feet in Vermont) has sugar maple, yellow birch, beech, white pine, hemlock, red spruce, white ash, black birch, basswood, black cherry and red maple. Montane spruce-fir forest begins around 2,500 feet in northern Vermont and 2,800 feet in the south; it has balsam fir, red spruce, mountain-ash and mountain paper birch.

Winter (no leaves)

The Virginia Tech factsheets describe twigs and buds, so these are the clues in winter, at any age:

TreeTwig and bud clues
Sugar MapleOpposite; slender, shiny brown twigs with pale lenticels; brown, very sharp-pointed terminal buds with tight scales.
Red MapleOpposite; reddish, lustrous twigs; blunt, green or reddish buds with several loose scales; V-shaped leaf scars with three bundle scars.
Striped MapleOpposite; smooth twigs, green turning red-brown; reddish, narrowly egg-shaped, stalked buds.
White AshOpposite; stout, gray-olive-green hairless twigs; large brown terminal bud with leathery scales; round leaf scars notched at the top.
American BeechAlternate; very slender zigzag light-brown twigs; long (about 3/4 inch) slender, "cigar-shaped" buds that stand away from the twig.
Yellow BirchAlternate; slender twigs that smell of wintergreen when broken; reddish-brown, sharply pointed buds with fringed scale edges.
Paper BirchAlternate; slender dull red-brown twigs with pale lenticels, no wintergreen smell, no terminal bud; sticky lateral buds.
American HophornbeamAlternate; slender reddish-brown twigs; small plump buds with finely grooved scales; clusters of male catkins at the branch tips; hop-like fruit sacs may hang on.
Black CherryAlternate; slender reddish-brown twigs with bitter-almond smell; very small glossy-scaled buds; small semicircular leaf scars.
Northern Red OakAlternate; stout red-brown hairless twigs; clustered large, conical terminal buds.
Quaking AspenAlternate; slender hairless reddish-brown twigs, often with a gray waxy film; conical reddish-brown buds.
ConifersEvergreen, so use the needles; see the first sort above.
