Solitary and Other Native Bees

beneficialanimal

The bees of Vermont other than the social bumble bees (Bumble Bees): mining bees, sweat bees, leafcutter bees, carpenter bees and many more, most of them small and solitary.

Also called
Native bees, Mining bees, Sweat bees, Leafcutter bees, Carpenter bees
Kind
insect
Native to Vermont
yes
Legal status
Many native bee species are of conservation concern; 43 were named Species of Greatest Conservation Need in the 2025 Wildlife Action Plan (search summary). Honeybees are not native. (checked 2026-10-30)

Names

About 4,000 bee species are native to North America and about 275 are found in Vermont (Landowner's Guide); a search summary of the Vermont Wild Bee Survey puts Vermont's total above 350 and says most are small, solitary and poorly understood. Honeybees are not native and are relatively unimportant pollinators in natural settings (Guide).

Range and habitat

Bees need nesting sites, overwintering sites and the plants they are adapted to. About 20 percent are pollen specialists that use one plant family, genus or species.

  • Ground nesters (mining bees, sweat bees): tunnels in sandy, silty or loamy soil, usually flat or gently sloping and sparsely vegetated.
  • Cavity nesters (about a quarter of the bees): twigs, acorns, snail shells, tree trunks and other wood; many seal the nest with chewed leaves (leafcutter bees). Large carpenter bees dig into sound wood and occur only in the extreme south of Vermont.
  • Winter: adult bees do not migrate; they shelter in underground cavities, hollow twigs and similar places, thought to be dry, protected and stable in temperature.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

Not described in the sources read: the Guide groups bees by nesting habit and does not give identification marks. Record these bees as "native bees" and photograph them from several angles.

Signs on the land (tracks, scat, browse, nests, holes, calls)

Small round nest holes in bare soil, banks, hedgerow soil and forest roads; leaf-cut notches and leaf-plugged holes in wood; bees on flowers.

Role in the forest (what it eats, what eats it, what it needs)

Pollinate blueberry, blackberry, apple and serviceberry, which feed bear, deer, turkey and moose. Plants that do not give bees pollen or nectar are the wind-pollinated ones: beech, birches, oaks, grasses, sedges, ferns and horsetails (Guide) (American Beech, Yellow Birch, Northern Red Oak). Open wetlands give blueberries, cranberries, Labrador tea, water lilies, Joe-Pye weed and asters.

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

  • A patchwork of habitats with flowers all season, and places to nest. Chapter 31 of the Guide (recorded in Pollinator Habitat on Forest and Field): keep forest, forest with sunny openings, wetlands and old fields with a mix of flowering plants; keep flowers on field edges, lawn edges and hedgerows; the plants bees favor include goldenrod, aster, willows, the blueberry family and dogwood, with maples, willows and apples key early in spring; leave bare or sparse, uncompacted soil, dead wood, pithy stems and slash for nests; limit pesticides, especially dusts, pellets and neonicotinoids and anything applied to flowers.
  • For this group: most native bees nest alone in the ground or in wood: bare, sparsely vegetated and uncompacted soil, dead wood and pithy stems are the nest features the Guide names.
