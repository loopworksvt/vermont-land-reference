Names

Queen Anne's lace (wild carrot), Daucus carota, in the carrot family, Apiaceae.

Range and habitat

Not native: Go Botany labels it "Non-native" and records it as present in Vermont. It lists human-disturbed habitats and meadows and fields (the Flora Novae Angliae entry adds roadsides and lawns) and does not classify its wetland status. Minnesota Wildflowers gives origin as Europe and lists part shade or sun; open fields, roadsides, waste areas and woodland edges.

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Height: 10 to 150 cm.
  • Flowers: five-petaled, in a flower cluster (an umbel) 40 to 120 mm wide made of 10 to 60 smaller umbel branches, each flower on a stalk 3 to 10 mm long.
  • Leaves: compound and alternate, blades 50 to 150 mm long, cut so finely that the final segments are only 2 to 15 mm long and 0.5 to 4 mm wide; the lobes are themselves lobed.
  • Care with look-alikes: many wild plants of fields and wet ground also carry white umbrella-shaped flower clusters. Ask the county forester, a botanist or a local plant guide before handling or eating a plant.
  • Other notes (Go Botany): edible when young; the root quickly becomes woody and fibrous with age.

What helps it thrive (light, soil and moisture, and how it is kept)

  • Light and soil: grows in full sun; gravelly or sandy soils but also harder clay.
  • Life: usually a biennial: leaves and a rosette the first year, flowers and seed the second (Washington State's page says it can also act as an annual or short-lived perennial).
  • Cutting: the sources say mowing or pulling in mid to late summer, before seed set, is effective, and that mowing alone is a supplement; tilling prevents seedlings from taking root; burning encourages seeds to sprout; vigorous desirable plant cover can exclude it.
  • Caution: the sap may be toxic: wear gloves and protective clothing (same summaries). The note's own caution about white umbrella-shaped flowers stands.
