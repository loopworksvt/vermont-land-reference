Names

Eastern Newt, Notophthalmus viridescens.

Range and habitat

Both pages say red efts (juveniles) live on land for up to eight years and adults return to permanent water: beaver ponds, small lakes, man-made ponds or marshes. The newt is fairly common in hardwood forests but needs large mosaics of interconnected hardwoods and wetlands, and is hard to find in coniferous uplands and in the spruce-fir forests of the Northeast Kingdom (Atlas).

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Red eft (juvenile, on land): reddish-orange with slightly bumpy skin.
  • Adult (in water): olive green with a bright yellow belly marked by scattered black spots.
  • Both stages: a dark horizontal line through the eye and rows of red spots outlined in black along the back. Size to 5 inches (Vermont Fish & Wildlife); a large Vermont adult averages 4.2 inches (Atlas). Sexual maturity at about age 3.

Role and management

Aquatic larvae eat small invertebrates including insect larvae; efts eat snails, springtails and soil mites; adults eat mainly midge larvae and other immature aquatic insects but take any small invertebrate (Vermont Fish & Wildlife). The state page names no landowner advice; it credits the reptile and amphibian atlas project (begun 1995), biological inventories, a vernal pool study and large-scale land conservation, and mentions environmental legislation that protects critical habitats of vulnerable salamanders without naming the newt.

What helps it thrive (light, soil, shelter, food and the trees it uses)

  • Water, shade and cover (the Guide, chapter 30). Most reptiles and amphibians need water for some stage of their life: conserve streams, ponds, wetlands and vernal pools. Frogs, toads and most salamanders lay eggs in water; many breed in temporary vernal pools free of fish, where the young change from egg to larva to adult in one summer if the pool stays wet (dry years give few eggs or hatchlings). Keep intact forest buffers along water, rock walls, brush piles, downed trees and hollow stumps; leave intact leaf litter and rotten logs; leave coarse woody debris when working in the woods ('woods that look like city parks are not good habitat'); keep streams shaded and free of sediment with a naturally vegetated buffer for night-time feeding; abundant shade keeps the woods cool and moist.
  • This species: named in the Guide among Vermont's ten salamander species; salamanders eat worms, snails, slugs and insects and their larvae, and need leaf mold, decaying logs and moist spots under rocks on land.
