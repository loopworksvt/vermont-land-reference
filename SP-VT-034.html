Names

Timothy, Phleum pratense, a grass in the family Poaceae. Also called common timothy.

Range and habitat

Not native. Go Botany says it was introduced to the United States in 1720 as a pasture grass and is now widespread and sometimes weedy; it is found in meadows, fields and other human-maintained places, and it is present in Vermont. The fire-effects page calls it an introduced, cool-season perennial bunchgrass of Eurasian origin. In old fields in southwest Ohio it was most abundant in the middle stages of old-field succession (found in fields up to 50 years old but not in fields 90 years old).

How to identify (by season; look-alikes and the key difference)

  • Growth habit (UVM): a tall bunchgrass that heads out later than most other grasses. Leaves are rolled in the bud shoot and tend to twist.
  • Seedhead: a dense, spike-like panicle 30 to 160 mm long and only 5 to 10 mm wide (Go Botany); each tiny spikelet has "horns" (UVM), which Go Botany describes as short awns of 0.7 to 1.5 mm on the two glumes.
  • Leaf: blades up to 45 cm long and 4 to 10 mm wide, flat or slightly folded, hairless and rough to the touch. The ligule is membranous, 2 to 4 mm long (Go Botany) and notched near the front, at least on one side (UVM).
  • Base: the stem base is swollen into a bulb-like corm (UVM calls it a unique bulb at its roots).
  • Look-alikes: alpine timothy (Phleum alpinum) has longer glume awns (1.5 to 3 mm) and no swollen base (Go Botany). Meadow foxtail also has a spike-like head but heads out early and spreads by rhizomes (Meadow Foxtail); timothy heads out late and has the swollen corm at its base.
  • Where you cannot yet see a seedhead, see How to Identify Grasses.

Role in the field

A hay and pasture grass; this is why it was brought to North America. The fire-effects page calls it an intermediate competitor that colonizes disturbed areas by seed, and says the carbohydrate reserves held in the lower stems and corms matter for regrowth after the top is removed, with a critical period of moving food to the corms in early fall.

What helps it thrive (light, soil and moisture, and how it is kept)

  • Light: tolerates high shade but thrives in partial shade (FEIS).
  • Soil and water: grows best on well-drained, moist clay or loam soils (deep, fertile, loamy, silty and clayey); intolerant of alkaline or acidic soils (lower pH limit 4.5 to 5.0); medium to high water need and intolerant of drought; tolerates winter flooding for several weeks but only a few days in the growing season.
  • Keeping it: seedlings usually establish by the second growing season; stands are generally short-lived (4 to 5 years). It is not resistant to heavy grazing; for hay, cut a few days after maximum flowering and before seed forms; late-season mowing is described for humid zones. It is harmed by fire when actively growing in spring and summer but fairly fire tolerant when dormant.
  • Use to others: cover for game birds, small mammals and waterfowl; seeds eaten by songbirds and game birds.

Managing for it and living with it

The sources read give no mowing dates or heights for timothy in an unmanaged field. Nothing read treats it as a problem plant.

Legal status

Not on the Agency of Agriculture quarantine list as read on 2026-10-30. No other legal status was looked up.
